Culture & Research Philosophy

AIT Brain Lab studies humans, artificial intelligence, and technologies that improve human lives. We combine experiments, computational methods, and theory to understand problems and develop useful solutions. We value curiosity, careful reasoning, reproducible work, and honest reporting of what works and what does not.

Bring a question you care about; we develop the research direction together. We help assess prior work and set a scope that fits your preparation, interests, degree, and time. Guidance is structured early; you take greater ownership as your skills develop.

PhD and Master’s Expectations

PhD: Develop an Original Contribution

PhD students develop an original contribution to knowledge and become increasingly independent researchers. A contribution may be a new explanation, method, interaction technique, benchmark, dataset, or system that produces meaningful new knowledge through rigorous evaluation.

  • Develop a focused research question within the lab’s areas of expertise, together with the supervisor.
  • Establish what prior work leaves unanswered and why answering it matters.
  • Design and carry out studies that support a clear contribution, including careful analysis of limitations.
  • Gradually lead research decisions, writing, and communication of the work.

Originality can be a focused, valuable advance. It does not require a breakthrough at the start, and the supervisor may provide the initial direction.

Master’s: Own a Focused Project within a Larger Effort

Master’s students will usually join an ongoing PhD project or a grant-funded research project. This provides a clearer starting point, shared resources, and support from the research team.

  • Agree on a bounded piece of work with clear learning goals and deliverables.
  • Contribute through implementation, experiments, data preparation, evaluation, or a focused research question.
  • Understand and justify the methods used, evaluate the results, and explain the project’s limitations.
  • Take ownership of an identifiable contribution suitable for the student’s degree requirements.

Students contribute as developing researchers with their own learning goals and credit for their work. A related topic proposed by the student is also welcome when supervision and resources are available.

Shared standards, different scope. Both pathways require integrity, thoughtful evaluation, and clear communication. The scale of the project and the expected level of independence differ. Project roles, individual contributions, and authorship expectations should be discussed early and revisited as work develops. These lab expectations complement the formal requirements of each degree programme.

Research Areas

Our two primary pillars connect understanding human behaviour with building reliable, adaptive AI agents. PhD students develop original contributions; master’s students usually own a focused part of a larger effort.

Primary Research Pillars

Our work illustrates the lab’s contributions; selected reading adds methods and evaluation approaches from the wider field. Benchmark success still needs validation for the people and settings a project aims to serve.

  1. Human Behaviour and Human–AI Interaction

    How do people behave, how accurately can agents model them, and how can AI strengthen human understanding, agency, and capabilities?

    • Human behaviour simulation: Model preferences, personalities, biases, memory, and cognitive load. Validate simulations against real human behaviour, beyond whether they appear believable.
    • Human biases and AI behaviour: Study how people and AI influence one another, including sycophancy, trust, negotiation, and decision-making.
    • Tools for human agency and well-being: Develop and evaluate interactions that support human control, cognition, attention regulation, habit formation, and independent judgment.

    Our work

    • From Overload to Convergence · CHI 2026 — Studies cognitive load in multi-issue human–AI negotiation and evaluates a Bayesian visualisation to help people identify promising agreements while retaining control.
    • Too Nice to Tell the Truth · ACL 2026 — Benchmarks role-playing language models and finds that more agreeable personas are associated with more sycophantic responses in most tested models.
    • Interactive Mindfulness and Attention Regulation · CHI 2017 — Develops a framework for interactive mindfulness tools that detect attention and provide feedback to support attention self-regulation without dedicated sensors.

    Start with a simulation benchmark and a human study

    • SimBench · ICLR 2026 · Benchmark — Compares human-behaviour simulation across 20 datasets, revealing limits in reproducing diverse choices and specific demographic groups.
    • Generative AI and critical thinking · CHI 2025 · Human study — Surveys knowledge workers about confidence and cognitive effort during AI use, motivating tools for independent judgment without establishing causal skill loss.
    Further reading: individual behaviour, negotiation, and agency
    • Multi-turn human behaviour simulation · ACL 2026 — Benchmarks agents against real shopping actions, distinguishing believable behaviour from accurate human prediction.
    • HugAgent · EMNLP 2026 · Benchmark — Uses questionnaires and think-aloud interviews to test predictions of individual beliefs and belief changes, rather than population-average responses.
    • Human Behavior Atlas · ICLR 2026 · Benchmark — Unifies text, audio, and visual tasks for psychological and social behaviour understanding, supporting evaluation across tasks and modalities.
    • PrefBench · 2026 preprint — Tests negotiation with hidden buyer preferences, showing that frequent agreement can coexist with weak seller outcomes.

  2. Reliable, Adaptive, and Accountable AI Agents

    How can agents use knowledge, memory, and uncertainty to act effectively, remain truthful, and make their decisions open to scrutiny?

    • Grounded reasoning: Combine language models with knowledge graphs, ontologies, rules, tools, and verification. Measure remaining errors; structure alone does not guarantee correctness.
    • Memory and adaptation: Learn what to remember, retrieve, clarify, explain, or defer, based on user needs, preferences, and uncertainty.
    • Honesty and accountability: Study behaviour under conflicting incentives and build benchmarks that separate factual knowledge, reported beliefs, and actual decisions.

    Start with a technique and a benchmark

    • APEX-MEM · ACL 2026 · Technique — Combines an ontology-based property graph, preserved event history, and retrieval-time conflict resolution for evolving conversational memory.
    • AMemGym · ICLR 2026 · Benchmark — Evaluates memory-driven personalisation through interactive conversations with structured, evolving simulated user states.
    Further reading: learning, grounding, and accountability
    • Memory-R1 · ACL 2026 · Technique — Uses reinforcement learning to teach agents when to add, update, delete, or retain memories and how to use them for answers.
    • AgentFlow · ICLR 2026 · Technique — Trains a planner within multi-turn interactions while coordinating execution, verification, and response generation through evolving memory.
    • KG-Agent · ACL 2025 — Combines knowledge-graph tools, an executor, and memory to support autonomous multi-hop question answering.
    • Memory-enhanced prompt optimisation · EMNLP Industry 2025 — Optimises memory and response prompts using module-level error attribution and selectively collected human feedback.
    • KnownLieBench · 2026 preprint — Verifies an agent’s factual knowledge before testing whether conflicting incentives lead it to make false claims.
    • Auditable negotiation beliefs · 2026 preprint — Distils explicit opponent-belief reports into a smaller model and tests whether those reported beliefs actually influence its actions.

    Also: PolicyKG: institutional policies and SHACL knowledge graphs.

Connecting the pillars: Our sales-agent project asks how an agent can understand and adapt to a customer while remaining truthful and preserving informed choice. We evaluate customer understanding, suitability, and control alongside agent performance.

Additional Research Directions

Medical AI and biomedical sensing retain their own scientific questions, methods, and evaluation needs.

  1. Medical Vision–Language AI

    Our current deep-learning interest is medical visual question answering (VQA): answering questions about medical images using both visual and language information.

    • Methods: Improve how models connect image evidence with questions and answers.
    • Data and evaluation: Develop clinically grounded datasets and assess accuracy, robustness, generalisation, and clinically meaningful errors.
    • Reliable assistance: Study evidence grounding and uncertainty, including when a model should defer to a clinician.

    Related work: SpineQA · VG-CALF

  2. Physiological Computing and Biomedical Sensing

    How can signals from the brain and body support useful interfaces and health-related measurement? This area brings together signal processing, machine learning, sensing, and interaction design.

    • EEG and brain–computer interfaces: Develop and evaluate brain-driven communication tools, including SSVEP, P300, and hybrid BCI spellers.
    • Raman spectroscopy: Investigate sensing and modelling approaches for non-invasive glucose measurement, with attention to calibration, confounds, and validation.

    Demo: BCI speller

Choosing a Project and Sharing the Work

Students from different disciplines are welcome. Together, we agree on the question, responsibilities, support, and milestones around your interests and goals.

Applications and prototypes can be valuable research tools and project outcomes. For a research contribution, we also ask what new capability, evidence, or understanding the work provides beyond building the application.

We aim to share strong research in suitable journals and conferences, including venues such as CHI, ACL, NeurIPS, and relevant specialist venues. Publication plans depend on the contribution, the student’s goals, and programme requirements. We expect careful work and thoughtful revision; acceptance decisions are outside our control.

See our Research Guide for practical help with developing questions, evaluating novelty, planning studies, and communicating results.
