From Research Idea to Top-Tier Publication
A practical, self-paced guide for CHI · ACL · NeurIPS
Slide 1 of 12
01 · Start here

Research is not just building something.

A research paper should help other people understand something they did not understand before.

Important problem→Clear question→Convincing evidence→New knowledge

Build

Make a chatbot, model, tool, or interface.

Research

Find out what it teaches us, why it works, when it fails, or how it changes people’s experience.

The test: If the application disappeared tomorrow, what would the paper still teach the research community?

02 · Ideation

Good ideas can come from several places.

You do not need to wait for a flash of genius. Start with something you notice, then turn it into a question.

1. Notice a surprising failure

Example: an AI tutor gives correct answers, but students still misunderstand the concept.

Question: Which explanations create a lasting misunderstanding?

2. Find a contradiction

One study says AI feedback builds confidence; another says it makes people overconfident.

Question: What conditions explain the difference?

3. Find a blind spot

A language model works well on standard English but fails on code-switching.

Question: Which language patterns trigger errors?

4. Challenge an assumption

People assume more explanations always help.

Question: When does extra explanation overwhelm users?

5. Borrow a lens from another field

Use a theory of cognitive load to understand AI-assisted decision-making.

Question: Does the theory explain which interface choices help?

6. Revisit a strong paper

Reproduce its key result, then test an important limitation or untested condition.

Question: Does the finding still hold outside the original setup?

Easy starting routine: Pick one interesting paper → explain its main finding → identify one limitation → propose one test that could change what we know.
03 · Ideation examples

Good idea vs. weak idea

Weak

“I will build an AI mental-health chatbot.”

Why weak: a broad application, unclear research question, no identified gap, and no claim about what we will learn.

Stronger

“When does reflective AI feedback help users reconsider negative self-talk, and when does it make them defend it?”

Why stronger: a focused phenomenon, a contrast that can be tested, and potential insights into interaction design.

Weak

“Use GPT with a knowledge graph to improve answers.”

Why weak: combining two technologies is not, by itself, a research contribution.

Stronger

“Which kinds of knowledge-graph constraints prevent unsupported answers, and what errors remain?”

Why stronger: it names a mechanism, measurable outcomes, and an opportunity for error analysis.

Remember: A good idea is not necessarily complicated. It is important, specific, feasible, testable, and likely to teach us something non-obvious.
04 · Novelty

How do I know whether my idea is novel?

Novelty is a comparison with the closest existing work—not a feeling, and not simply the absence of a search result.

Find 3–5 closest papers→Compare claims and evidence→State the exact difference→Explain why it matters
Bad novelty claim

“No one has used GPT-5 for this task.”

Why weak: a model swap may not change the research question or knowledge gained. Search terms may also miss equivalent work.

Better novelty claim

“Prior systems measure answer accuracy; we test whether specific linguistic error types cause users to trust incorrect advice.”

Why stronger: it distinguishes the research question, the evidence, and the insight—if the closest papers really do leave that gap open.

Bad novelty claim

“Our interface has a new color scheme.”

Why weak: visual difference alone rarely creates a meaningful HCI contribution.

Better novelty claim

“We compare two feedback designs and discover when users notice, challenge, or accept AI mistakes.”

Why stronger: it can yield transferable evidence about human–AI interaction, not just a new screen.

Write this sentence: “Unlike the closest work A and B, we investigate X; this matters because Y.” Then verify every part against the papers.
05 · Related work

Find papers—and read them with a purpose.

Find the right papers

  • Start with recent accepted papers at your target venue.
  • Search Google Scholar, ACL Anthology, ACM Digital Library, and OpenReview.
  • Follow references backward and citations forward.

Read in three passes

  1. Title, abstract, figures, conclusion.
  2. Question, contribution, methods, results.
  3. Deep-read the closest papers, including appendices and code when available.

Make a comparison table

For each closest paper, record: question, contribution, method, evidence, limitations, and how your idea differs.

Avoid this: listing papers one by one. Do this: synthesize what is known, where findings disagree, what remains unresolved, and exactly where your work fits.

06 · Methodology

Bad methodology: what does it look like?

Bad

Question and metric do not match

Claim: “The tool improves trust.” Measure: task completion time only.

Problem: speed does not establish trust.

Bad

Weak or unfair baseline

Compare a tuned new method with an outdated baseline using different data or resources.

Problem: the gain may come from the comparison setup.

Bad

Confounds and leakage

Test examples leak into training, or one study condition gets easier tasks.

Problem: you cannot tell what caused the result.

Bad

Unclear or tiny sample

Recruit whoever is available, then generalize to all users without justification.

Problem: uncertainty and selection bias are ignored.

Bad

Vague procedure

“We asked an LLM to evaluate the outputs” with no prompt, rubric, validation, or reliability check.

Problem: readers cannot judge validity or reproduce the work.

Bad

Claiming causation from correlation

Users who like a tool perform better, so the paper claims liking caused better performance.

Problem: other explanations may account for the association.

Before implementation, specify: research question, unit of analysis, data/participants, sampling, conditions, measures, baselines, analysis plan, ethics, and threats to validity.
07 · Methodology by venue

Choose methods that fit the claim.

VenueCore questionMethodological priorities
CHIWhat do we learn about people and interaction?Relevant HCI literature/theory, justified participants, credible study protocol, careful qualitative and/or quantitative analysis, design implications.
ACLWhat language capability or limitation is revealed?Clear task and corpora, valid data splits, annotation quality, competitive baselines, linguistic phenomena, reproducible model/prompt settings.
NeurIPSWhat method or general ML insight is advanced?Strong baselines, fair compute, ablations, uncertainty, multiple seeds where appropriate, robustness and reproducibility.
One rule: Design the experiment around the claim you want to make. Do not choose a convenient metric first and invent a claim afterward.
08 · Experiments

Each experiment should answer a question.

1. Main comparison

Question: Does it work better than strong alternatives?

Use fair datasets, splits, settings, metrics, and competitive baselines.

2. Ablation / mechanism

Question: Which component matters, and why?

Remove or vary components to test their role.

3. Robustness / generalization

Question: Does it still work under realistic changes?

Test different domains, languages, users, prompts, data conditions, or distribution shifts.

4. Human / expert validation

Question: Does the result matter in practice?

Use a justified study design, suitable measures, and a transparent analysis.

5. Failure analysis

Question: Where does it fail, and what pattern explains the failure?

Inspect representative and systematic failures, not only success examples.

6. Efficiency and trade-offs

Question: What do we pay for the improvement?

Report cost, latency, compute, usability, fairness, or other relevant trade-offs.

There is no magic number of experiments. Include the evidence needed to support every major claim—and avoid experiments that add tables but no new understanding.
09 · Analysis

What reviewers look for beyond the headline score.

A strong paper explains not only whether a result happened, but also why, when, for whom, and at what cost.

Error analysis

Group failures into meaningful categories. Which errors dominate? Which are new? What causes them? Show representative examples and counts.

Linguistic analysis

For language work: examine negation, ambiguity, coreference, long-distance dependencies, pragmatics, morphology, code-switching, or other phenomena relevant to the claim.

Robustness analysis

Vary domains, languages, prompts, noise, populations, or assumptions. Identify which changes break the result and which do not.

Ablation and sensitivity

Remove components or vary key settings. Determine whether the proposed mechanism actually explains the gain.

Subgroups and individual differences

For CHI: how do experience, goals, ability, context, or interaction style change outcomes? Avoid unsupported subgroup claims.

Qualitative and behavioral insight

Use interviews, interaction traces, think-aloud data, or expert review to explain patterns that a score cannot reveal.

Uncertainty and significance

Report variability and uncertainty. Distinguish statistical significance from practical importance; do not treat “not significant” as proof of no effect.

Trade-offs, bias, and limitations

Who benefits or loses? What resources are required? Where is performance uneven? What alternative explanation remains?

Write every analysis as: Observation (what happened) → Interpretation (why it may have happened) → Implication (what researchers should learn or do next).
10 · Contributions

What kind of contribution are you making?

Method or model

A new algorithm, architecture, objective, or training procedure that shows demonstrated value.

Empirical knowledge

A robust, non-obvious finding about people, language, or models.

Theory or framework

An explanation or predictive account that clarifies a phenomenon.

Design or system

An interaction technique that produces transferable design knowledge—not just a demo.

Dataset or benchmark

A resource that enables meaningful new research or exposes a neglected capability.

Evaluation method

A more valid, reliable, or useful way to measure something important.

Application insight

A demanding real-world setting that reveals a broader principle or limitation.

Replication or reassessment

A careful test that confirms, qualifies, or challenges an important claim.

Focus: One clear central contribution is usually stronger than a long list of loosely connected claims. Technical difficulty is not the same as research value.
11 · Rejection and student pitfalls

Common reasons papers are rejected

Unclear novelty or significance

Reviewers cannot see what is new, why it matters, or how it differs from the closest work.

Wrong venue fit

An application demo is presented as an HCI insight, or an API integration as a new ML method.

Weak evidence

Unfair baselines, unsuitable metrics, missing controls, leakage, or insufficient analysis.

Missing insight

Only headline scores are reported; no error analysis, robustness checks, or explanation of why results happen.

Overclaiming

Conclusions go beyond the evidence or ignore uncertainty and limitations.

Unclear reporting

Readers cannot understand or reproduce the procedure, or ethics and data provenance are unclear.

Common student traps

  • Reading forever: define a question, build a comparison matrix, then run a small pilot.
  • Building without a claim: state what the app lets you investigate and what others will learn.
  • Only a tiny score gain: check variance, baselines, cost, robustness, and mechanism—never invent a story around noise.
  • Getting stuck quietly: report what you tried, the evidence, the exact blocker, and the options you considered.
  • Scope too big: choose one research question, one central contribution, and one decisive experiment.
12 · Your next step

Prepare a one-page research pitch.

Bring a concrete argument to your supervisor—not just a topic or a list of ideas.

  1. Problem: What important situation or challenge are you studying?
  2. Closest work: What are the three most relevant papers, and what did they establish?
  3. Gap: What remains unanswered, and why does it matter?
  4. Research question: What exactly will you investigate?
  5. Contribution: What new knowledge or capability could result?
  6. Evidence: What baseline, measure, dataset/participants, and analysis would convince a skeptical reviewer?
  7. Risk: What is the biggest uncertainty, and what cheap pilot can test it?
Final reviewer test
What is new? Why should anyone care? Is the comparison fair? What did you learn from failures? Why do the results happen? When might the claim stop holding?
