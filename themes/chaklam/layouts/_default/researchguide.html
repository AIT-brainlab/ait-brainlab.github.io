WELCOME

Ultimate Guide to Your Research Success

By Chaklam

Research is not just building an application. It is asking a meaningful question, finding convincing evidence, and contributing knowledge that other people find useful.

The research test: What new understanding will other researchers, designers, or practitioners gain from your paper—and why would it matter to them?

HOW TO THINK

Six levels of thinking — same topic, deeper questions

Follow each column downward: the question becomes less about recalling facts and more about understanding causes and making better decisions.

BloomPersonal life: selling more sushiAI that helps people manage emotionsAI-simulated humans in research
Remember
Information		How many sushi boxes did I sell last week?What emotions do people report when using an AI support chatbot?What answers do AI-generated survey participants give?
Understand
Information		Which sushi sells most, and at what times?How does the chatbot respond when users say they feel sad, angry, or anxious?How similar are those answers to responses from real people?
Apply
Knowledge		Can I use sales patterns to adjust how much sushi I prepare each day?Can I apply established emotion-regulation strategies to the chatbot's responses?Can I use AI-generated responses to test a survey before giving it to real participants?
Analyze
Knowledge		Why do some customers buy once but not return: price, taste, freshness, or variety?Why does the same AI response calm one person but upset another?On which questions, groups, or situations do AI-generated answers differ most from real people's answers?
Evaluate
Wisdom		Which change actually increases repeat purchases without reducing quality or profit?When should AI offer advice, ask a question, adapt its tone, or avoid intervening?For which research questions can simulated answers be useful, and when must we ask real people?
Create
Wisdom		How can I design and test a sushi experience that makes customers return regularly without relying on discounts?How can we design and evaluate AI support that adapts to a person's emotions, values, and culture to help them regulate emotions appropriately?How can we design a research method that uses AI simulations for early exploration but checks important findings with real participants?
THINKING TOOLS

Thinking tools

Original question: “Can an AI chatbot help students cope with stress?”

Thinking toolHow the question changesWhy it helps
Challenge assumptionsCan chatbot advice sometimes increase stress instead of reducing it?Challenges the belief that more AI help is always better.
First principlesWhat do stressed students actually need before we decide to use a chatbot?Starts from the human need, not the technology.
Systems thinkingHow do deadlines, sleep, friends, and chatbot use affect stress together?Looks at interacting causes.
Second-order thinkingIf students rely on a chatbot for months, does their ability to cope independently change?Investigates longer-term effects.
InversionWhen might a chatbot make a stressed student feel worse?Identifies risks and failure conditions.
Root-cause analysisIs the main problem a lack of advice, or an overwhelming workload?Investigates the underlying cause.
Thinking by analogyWhat can chatbot designers learn from how peer-support groups listen?Transfers ideas from another domain.
Compare alternativesWhen is a chatbot more useful than journaling or talking with a friend?Defines a meaningful comparison.
Stakeholder perspectivesWould students, counselors, and instructors define “helpful” in the same way?Reveals competing needs and evaluation criteria.

Thinking tools ↑ help us climb toward deeper questions and wiser judgment.

Novelty

NOVELTY

Novelty is judged by the research community

Novelty is not about what feels new to you. Other researchers decide whether your idea is new relative to published work, interesting to the field, and important enough to change understanding or practice. The only credible starting point is to read closely related papers.

IdeaNovel?Why?
First stress chatbot at my universityNoA new location alone teaches little beyond prior work.
Use a newer LLM for emotion supportNoA model swap alone is not a research insight.
Discover when AI advice makes people feel less in controlPotentiallyA meaningful effect, if not already established.
Generate 10,000 simulated survey respondentsNoMore simulated responses alone do not establish validity.
Identify which questions AI-simulated people answer differently from real peoplePotentiallyCould show when simulations should not be trusted.
Change a dashboard's colorNoAppearance alone rarely yields new understanding.
Find when AI reminders help beginners but distract experienced studentsPotentiallyCould reveal an important boundary condition.

EXERCISE · RELATED WORK

Find a gap from three related papers

Simple question: Can AI give people helpful emotional support? Compare what these real papers actually test.

PaperWhat did they study?What did they not establish?
A · Rashkin et al. (2019)Can chatbots reply in ways that sound understanding?Whether users feel better afterward.
B · Liu et al. (2021)Can AI learn different ways of offering emotional support?Which approach helps which person in real life.
C · Tu et al. (2022)Can AI improve supportive replies by tracking emotions and combining strategies?Whether better replies lead to lasting benefits for users.
Proposed study: culture × values × humorWhen does humor help or hurt emotional support from AI across cultures, and does matching a person’s values change the outcome?Compare culturally adapted / generic responses, with and without humor; measure feeling understood, offense, and willingness to continue. Check whether differences depend on personal values.

Why this is more interesting: Earlier work builds empathetic replies and support strategies. Our question asks when the same joke comforts one person but offends another, depending on culture and personal values. Candidate idea, not verified novelty: search newer humor, culture, and personalization papers before claiming a gap.

Methodology & evidence

Make the question testable and the evidence trustworthy.

METHODOLOGY

What makes a good methodology?

Example question: Do reflective AI prompts help students regulate emotions better than direct AI advice?

ComponentWhat to specify
Independent variable (IV)Type of AI response: reflective questions vs direct advice.
Dependent variable (DV)Emotion-regulation outcome, e.g., validated self-report measure, plus appropriate behavioral outcomes.
Confounding variablesBaseline distress, prior AI use, expectations, participant characteristics.
Controls and comparisonSame tasks, duration, interface, and access; suitable non-AI or alternative-AI baseline.
RandomizationHow participants are assigned; order counterbalancing if using both conditions.
Participants / datasetsWho is eligible, sample-size rationale, language, recruitment, and exclusion criteria.
Tasks and proceduresExactly what participants see/do, timing, consent, ethics, and safeguards.
Analysis methodsStatistical comparisons and uncertainty; qualitative themes; error categories; subgroup patterns.
ReproducibilityMaterials, prompts, versions, scripts, coding rules, and limitations.
Venue fitCHI: human behavior, design, and experience. ACL: language phenomena, baselines, error and linguistic analysis.

Which analyses answer which questions?

AnalysisPurpose
Comparison / baselineIs it better than a fair alternative?
AblationWhich component causes the improvement?
Error analysisWhere and why does it fail?
Linguistic analysisDo negation, sarcasm, or phrasing affect results?
RobustnessDoes it hold across people, prompts, or datasets?
Qualitative analysisWhat do users experience, and why?
CONTRIBUTIONS

Different research contributions need different evidence

Before choosing a method, ask: What new knowledge will others gain? Not every study needs an IV, DV, or random assignment.

ContributionWhat others learnExampleSuitable evidenceWhen do IV / DV help?
Controlled experimentWhether a change causes a differenceDo supportive AI replies make students feel more understood than neutral replies?Groups, measured outcomes, fair comparison, statisticsYes for a causal experiment: IV = what changes; DV = what you measure.
Design contributionHow to design better systems, and whyDesign an AI tool that helps people name emotions while keeping control of decisionsDesign theory, prototype, user feedback, design lessonsNot automatically. Add a comparison experiment if you want to claim the design or framework causes an improvement.
Algorithm / methodA better way to solve a technical problemDetect emotionally harmful chatbot responsesStrong baselines, ablation, errors, robustnessOften useful: IV = new method vs baseline or with/without a component; DV = accuracy, speed, cost, or safety score. Human participants are not always needed.
Dataset / benchmarkA reliable resource for future researchA dataset of how people express frustration in different culturesSampling, labeling quality, coverage, documentationNot needed to collect and validate a dataset. Useful in a downstream experiment: IV = train with vs without the new dataset; DV = model accuracy or another score.
Qualitative insightHow people experience a phenomenonWhy students avoid using mental-health appsInterviews, systematic coding, themes, supporting quotesNot automatically. Add a comparison experiment if you want to claim the design or framework causes an improvement.
Conceptual frameworkA new way to explain or organize a problemA framework of when AI support helps or undermines autonomyGrounding in literature, coherent reasoning, empirical illustration where appropriateNot automatically. Add a comparison experiment if you want to claim the design or framework causes an improvement.

Rule: A methodology is good when it produces convincing evidence for that kind of contribution.

Dataset example: Making a culturally diverse emotion dataset requires good sampling and reliable labels. To test its usefulness, train the same model with and without that dataset (IV), then compare performance on a held-out test set (DV). Both kinds of evidence matter, but they answer different questions.

DESIGN RESEARCH

What if your paper contributes a new design?

Example: an AI emotion-regulation tool based on Self-Determination Theory (supporting people's sense of choice and control).

Research componentWhat a design paper could do
Research questionHow might AI help people reflect on difficult feelings without telling them what to do?
TheoryUse autonomy from Self-Determination Theory to explain why users should keep meaningful choices.
Design processInterview potential users, sketch alternatives, build a prototype, revise it based on feedback.
What is created?An interface that offers several supportive paths rather than a single instruction.
EvaluationObserve how people use it; ask what feels helpful, confusing, or controlling; analyze patterns.
IV / DV / randomizationNot required for this exploratory design study. Add them only if claiming one design causes better outcomes than another.
Contribution to othersReusable design principles—for example, how to offer choices without overwhelming users.

Important: This is a hypothetical teaching example, not a claim about a published paper. A working prototype alone is not enough; the paper must explain what others can learn from designing and studying it.

WORKED EXAMPLE · CONTROLLED EXPERIMENT

Does culturally matched humor help?

We want to know whether a change in the AI reply causes people to feel more understood.

ComponentConcrete example
Research questionDoes culturally appropriate humor help users feel supported, or can it backfire?
IVReply style: no humor / generic humor / culturally adapted humor.
DVFeeling understood (1–7), perceived offense (1–7), and willingness to continue.
ParticipantsRecruit participants from relevant cultural groups; measure personal values rather than guessing them.
ControlsSame stressful scenario, model, interface, approximate reply length, and task.
RandomizationRandomly assign reply condition; counterbalance order if each person sees several replies.
ConfoundersHumor preference, baseline distress, language fluency, and prior AI experience.
AnalysisCompare group scores; examine culture × humor interactions and cases where jokes offend.
ContributionEvidence about when humor helps, for whom, and why it can fail.

Hypothetical study design—not a published result. Use ethical safeguards when discussing distress.

WORKED EXAMPLE · ALGORITHM & DATASET

A new dataset or algorithm: what must we test?

ComponentNew datasetNew algorithm
Research questionCan we represent culturally different ways of expressing distress?Can a model better recognize indirect distress?
What is new?Carefully sampled, annotated cross-cultural examples.A method combining context and cultural cues.
IV in comparisonTraining with vs without the new dataset.New method vs strong baselines; with vs without each module.
DV in comparisonPerformance on a separate, held-out test set.Precision/recall/F1, robustness, cost, and fairness gaps.
Quality checksLabel agreement, consent, coverage, leakage checks.Fair training setup, baselines, hyperparameters.
AnalysisWhere does the dataset improve or hurt performance?Ablation, error, linguistic, cross-group and robustness analysis.
ContributionA reusable resource plus evidence of its quality and usefulness.A repeatable method plus evidence of when and why it works.

Key distinction: A dataset does not need an IV to exist. But an experiment testing whether that dataset improves a model does have an IV and DV.

Methodology componentWhat it means in this study
Research questionDo instructions telling a chatbot to be empathetic change the user experience?

DV (what is measured)Participant-reported experience, including perceived empathy / emotional response.

ProcedureInteract with assigned chatbot, then answer user-experience questions.

REAL PAPER · CoNLL 2026

Human vs AI empathy: a rating experiment

Read the original CoNLL paper. This is a comparison of responses, not a trial of therapy or long-term mental health.

Methodology componentWhat it means in this study
Research questionAre AI-generated replies judged as empathetic as human-written replies?
IV (what changes)Source of the reply: human writer versus language-model systems.
DV (what is measured)Human empathy judgments of replies.
Dataset / evaluatorsDialogue prompts and human evaluators (reported study scale: 2,000 prompts and 1,000 evaluators).
Baseline / controlHuman-written replies serve as an important comparison.
Assignment / blindingCheck the full paper for how replies were shown, ordered, and source labels handled; do not assume randomization.
ProcedureGenerate or collect replies to emotional situations; ask people to judge their empathy.
Possible confounders (our analysis)Reply length, topic, evaluator background, model familiarity, cultural interpretation.
AnalysisCompare empathy ratings across reply sources and emotional contexts.
Important limitationA high empathy rating does not prove that a reply improves real-world emotional health.
ReproducibilityPublish or describe prompts, response sources, rating instructions, sampling, and analysis.
EXERCISE · METHODOLOGY

Design a new study inspired by an ACL paper

Starting paper: EmpatheticDialogues (ACL 2019) studies whether AI replies sound empathetic. Your new question: “Do students feel more understood after an empathetic AI reply than after a neutral AI reply?”

Scenario: Students read the same short stressful situation and receive one of two AI replies. You design a new user experiment; this is not the original ACL paper's experiment.

ComponentGivenFill in
IV (what changes?)AI reply styleTwo conditions: __________ vs __________
DV (what is measured?)Feeling understoodHow will students rate this? __________
Control (what stays the same?)Same stressful situationWhat else stays equal? __________
Confounder (what else may affect the result?)Some students already like AIHow might you account for this? __________
AssignmentTwo groupsHow will you assign students? __________
AnalysisCompare the two groupsWhat scores or comments will you compare? __________

Example answer

IV: empathetic vs neutral wording. DV: average score on “I felt understood” (1–7). Control: same scenario, reply length, interface, and time. Confounder: previous AI experience; measure it and check whether groups differ. Assignment: randomly assign students. Analysis: compare average scores with uncertainty, then review open-ended comments for why students preferred one reply.

Students' ratings measure perceived understanding—not whether their mental health improved.
