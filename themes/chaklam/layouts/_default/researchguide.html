Ultimate Guide to Your Research Success

By Chaklam

Research is not just building an AI app. It is asking a worthwhile question, gathering convincing evidence, and helping others understand something new.

Think deeply→Find a meaningful question→Test it rigorously→Share useful insight
The research test: If your application disappeared tomorrow, what would your paper still teach others?

What top-tier research looks like (PDF)

Better questions

Information → Knowledge → Wisdom

Use these levels to improve your research question—not just to classify answers.

LevelExample: AI for emotion regulationWhat it gives us
InformationHow often do students use an AI emotion-regulation assistant?Describes what happens.
KnowledgeWhy does reflective AI feedback help some students but frustrate others?Explains patterns and mechanisms.
WisdomWhen should an assistant intervene, stay silent, or recommend human support?Supports decisions that account for context, values, and consequences.
Move your idea from “What happens?” to “Why?” and “When and what should we do?” Higher-level questions are not automatically novel or feasible.
Thinking beyond implementation

Bloom's Taxonomy: climb the pyramid

Research is hard because we must go beyond remembering facts and applying existing tools. We learn to analyze, evaluate, create—and exercise judgment.

CreateDesign and test a new interaction · wisdom-informed creation
EvaluateDecide when an AI intervention helps or harms · judgment
AnalyzeExplain why some people reject AI advice · knowledge
ApplyBuild an emotion-regulation prototype · applying knowledge
UnderstandExplain emotion-regulation strategies · information to understanding
RememberRecall definitions and prior findings · information
Important: Bloom's levels and information–knowledge–wisdom are different frameworks. These labels are teaching connections, not exact equivalences. Research aims for deeper understanding and wise judgment, supported by evidence—not simply a higher pyramid level.
Tools to generate stronger questions

Thinking tools

Start with an idea: “AI reminders will help students build better habits.” See how different thinking tools change the question.

ToolUse it immediately: a better question
Challenge assumptionsDo more reminders always improve habit formation—or can they reduce autonomy?
First principlesWhat actually drives lasting habit change: reminders, motivation, ability, or context?
Systems thinkingHow do reminders, stress, motivation, and missed goals influence each other over time?
Second-order thinkingIf reminders increase compliance this week, do they create dependence next month?
InversionWhat would make this habit assistant fail or even make habits worse?
Counterfactual thinkingWhat would change if the same student received no reminders—or could edit them?
You do not have to memorize the tools. Use one or two to transform a vague idea into an interesting question.
From observations to ideas

Where do good ideas come from?

Nine starting points. The label tells you which thinking tool can help.

1 · A real problem

First principles: Why do students abandon mental-health apps after one week?

2 · A surprising failure

Inversion / error analysis: Why does emotion classification fail on sarcasm?

3 · Contradictory findings

Challenge assumptions: Why does AI advice help in one study but cause over-reliance in another?

4 · Overlooked people or settings

Systems thinking: How does a simulated-human study change when real people face social pressure?

5 · A questionable assumption

Challenge assumptions: Does more personalized feedback always improve habit formation?

6 · Borrow a theory

First principles: Can self-determination theory explain why some AI nudges backfire?

7 · New capability

Second-order thinking: What happens when realistic simulated humans are used to replace pilot participants?

8 · Missing measurement

Inversion: What important harm does an average emotion-recognition score hide?

9 · Revisit a published result

Counterfactual thinking: Does the result persist without AI assistance or for a different population?

Workshop connection: choose one starting point and apply two thinking tools.
03 · Examples across domains

Weak ideas become stronger when they ask something specific.

DomainWeak ideaStronger research ideaWhy stronger?
Medical VQABuild a medical chatbot.Which image–question mismatches cause clinically dangerous answers, and can evidence grounding reduce them?Targets a failure mechanism and safety-relevant evaluation.
Small language modelsFine-tune a small LLM.When does distillation preserve reasoning but lose sensitivity to negation and uncertainty?Identifies a measurable trade-off and phenomenon.
HCI designMake a nicer dashboard.How does editable versus fixed AI feedback change users’ ability to correct mistaken recommendations?Compares interaction designs and human behavior.
Simulated humansGenerate 1,000 AI personas.Which human behavioral patterns do simulated participants reproduce, and which do they systematically miss?Tests validity rather than volume.
Human–AI interactionAdd explanations to an AI assistant.When do explanations improve calibrated reliance versus encourage blind acceptance?Tests conditions and mechanisms.
NLPCombine RAG with a graph.Which relation types reduce unsupported answers, and what errors remain?Isolates an explanatory mechanism.
These are candidate questions, not verified novel contributions. You must still inspect the closest papers.
04 · Novelty check

“New to me” is not the same as novel research.

Compare your question and contribution—not just your model name, dataset, or interface.

Weak novelty argumentStronger possibilityWhy?
First medical VQA app in our hospitalDiscover a previously unmeasured class of clinically consequential visual grounding failuresPotential generalizable insight, not just location.
Use a newer small LLMExplain a reproducible accuracy–latency–calibration trade-off across model sizesIdentifies conditions and mechanisms.
New colors in an HCI interfaceDemonstrate how editable feedback changes correction behaviorProduces interaction knowledge.
More synthetic participantsDevelop and validate a method to detect when simulations diverge from human behaviorAddresses construct validity.
LLM with another promptIdentify when prompt wording changes reliance and whyTests a meaningful phenomenon.
Find 3–5 closest papers→Compare their questions→Identify missing evidence→Explain the significance
Novelty sentence: “Prior work establishes ___. It remains unclear ___. We test ___, which matters because ___.” If prior work already answers it, refine your question.
10 · Contributions

What kind of contribution are you making?

Method or model

A new algorithm, architecture, objective, or training procedure that shows demonstrated value.

Empirical knowledge

A robust, non-obvious finding about people, language, or models.

Theory or framework

An explanation or predictive account that clarifies a phenomenon.

Design or system

An interaction technique that produces transferable design knowledge—not just a demo.

Dataset or benchmark

A resource that enables meaningful new research or exposes a neglected capability.

Evaluation method

A more valid, reliable, or useful way to measure something important.

Application insight

A demanding real-world setting that reveals a broader principle or limitation.

Replication or reassessment

A careful test that confirms, qualifies, or challenges an important claim.

Focus: One clear central contribution is usually stronger than a long list of loosely connected claims. Technical difficulty is not the same as research value.
Avoidable mistakes

Common traps of students

Starting with a tool

“I want to use an LLM” without a meaningful question.

Calling implementation novelty

“Nobody built this exact app” without comparing the closest research.

Shopping-list literature review

Summarizing papers one by one without identifying agreement, contradictions, or gaps.

Weak evidence

Claiming a habit app changes behavior after only measuring whether users liked it.

Only reporting averages

Ignoring error analysis, subgroups, negative cases, and robustness.

Overclaiming

“Simulated humans behave like real humans” after testing only a few tasks.

Workshop connection: identify one trap your idea could fall into, and explain how you will avoid it.
Reading papers

Related work is synthesis—not a shopping list

Shopping list: “Paper A did X. Paper B did Y. Paper C did Z.” Synthesis: “Together these papers show X, but disagree on Y and leave Z unanswered.”

Use a comparison table to find a research gap. Example rows below are hypothetical teaching examples—not actual citations.

Example paperWhat was studied?What was learned?What is still missing?
AAI prompts for daily habitsShort-term engagement increasedLong-term autonomy?
BPersonalized emotion-regulation suggestionsUsers liked relevant suggestionsWhen suggestions feel intrusive?
CAI-simulated user reactionsSome preference patterns matchedAgreement with real behavior under stress?
Your studyWhen do personalized AI nudges support versus undermine autonomy?To be testedCompare conditions and explain differences
Do this with real papers: find the 3–5 closest studies; compare questions, participants, methods, results, and limitations. A blank cell is not automatically a gap—explain why the missing answer matters.
Designing trustworthy studies

What makes a good methodology?

Your method must answer your question, be transparent enough to reproduce, and fit the evidence expected by your venue.

What to planExample: AI-assisted emotion regulation
Research question and construct validityAre you measuring emotional improvement, engagement, autonomy, or just satisfaction? Define each.
ReproducibilityDocument participant selection, prompts, model version, conditions, procedure, coding rules, and analysis.
Venue fitCHI: interaction design, human outcomes, study rigor, qualitative insight. ACL: language phenomena, strong baselines, datasets, linguistic/error analysis, generalization.
Comparison / controlsCompare AI feedback with no feedback, fixed feedback, or a relevant existing approach.
Ablation studiesRemove personalization or reflection prompts to learn what causes any gain.
Error / failure analysisInspect cases where suggestions are mistimed, misunderstood, or harmful.
Robustness / sensitivityTest different user groups, moods, prompts, and follow-up periods where feasible.
Ethics and limitationsProtect sensitive data; avoid unsupported therapeutic claims; report uncertainty and boundaries.
Ask: Could another researcher repeat this? Would my evidence support the claim? What analysis explains why—not just whether—it works?
07 · Methodology by venue

Choose methods that fit the claim.

VenueCore questionMethodological priorities
CHIWhat do we learn about people and interaction?Relevant HCI literature/theory, justified participants, credible study protocol, careful qualitative and/or quantitative analysis, design implications.
ACLWhat language capability or limitation is revealed?Clear task and corpora, valid data splits, annotation quality, competitive baselines, linguistic phenomena, reproducible model/prompt settings.
NeurIPSWhat method or general ML insight is advanced?Strong baselines, fair compute, ablations, uncertainty, multiple seeds where appropriate, robustness and reproducibility.
One rule: Design the experiment around the claim you want to make. Do not choose a convenient metric first and invent a claim afterward.
Experiments that teach us something

What evidence would support your claim?

Example claim: “Editable AI reflections help students regulate emotions without undermining autonomy.”

Formal analysisWhat students could actually doWhat they learn
Comparative evaluationCompare editable AI feedback, fixed AI feedback, and an appropriate control.Whether the design changes relevant outcomes.
Ablation studyRemove editing or personalization separately.Which feature matters.
Error / failure analysisGroup unhelpful reflections into mistiming, misunderstanding, tone, or other categories.Where and why the approach fails.
Robustness analysisTest different emotions, user characteristics, or prompt variants.When the result holds or breaks.
Qualitative / behavioral analysisInterview participants and inspect interaction logs.Why people react differently.
You do not need every analysis. Choose the ones needed to justify your research question and claim.
09 · Analysis

What reviewers look for beyond the headline score.

A strong paper explains not only whether a result happened, but also why, when, for whom, and at what cost.

Error analysis

Group failures into meaningful categories. Which errors dominate? Which are new? What causes them? Show representative examples and counts.

Linguistic analysis

For language work: examine negation, ambiguity, coreference, long-distance dependencies, pragmatics, morphology, code-switching, or other phenomena relevant to the claim.

Robustness analysis

Vary domains, languages, prompts, noise, populations, or assumptions. Identify which changes break the result and which do not.

Ablation and sensitivity

Remove components or vary key settings. Determine whether the proposed mechanism actually explains the gain.

Subgroups and individual differences

For CHI: how do experience, goals, ability, context, or interaction style change outcomes? Avoid unsupported subgroup claims.

Qualitative and behavioral insight

Use interviews, interaction traces, think-aloud data, or expert review to explain patterns that a score cannot reveal.

Uncertainty and significance

Report variability and uncertainty. Distinguish statistical significance from practical importance; do not treat “not significant” as proof of no effect.

Trade-offs, bias, and limitations

Who benefits or loses? What resources are required? Where is performance uneven? What alternative explanation remains?

Write every analysis as: Observation (what happened) → Interpretation (why it may have happened) → Implication (what researchers should learn or do next).
12 · Your next step

Prepare a one-page research pitch.

Bring a concrete argument to your supervisor—not just a topic or a list of ideas.

  1. Problem: What important situation or challenge are you studying?
  2. Closest work: What are the three most relevant papers, and what did they establish?
  3. Gap: What remains unanswered, and why does it matter?
  4. Research question: What exactly will you investigate?
  5. Contribution: What new knowledge or capability could result?
  6. Evidence: What baseline, measure, dataset/participants, and analysis would convince a skeptical reviewer?
  7. Risk: What is the biggest uncertainty, and what cheap pilot can test it?
Final reviewer test
What is new? Why should anyone care? Is the comparison fair? What did you learn from failures? Why do the results happen? When might the claim stop holding?

Worked example · Before you begin

Example: from a crazy thought to a research plan

Domain: Emotion regulation / HCI. Crazy thought: “What if an AI assistant sometimes refuses to give advice?”

StepWorked answer
Information questionHow often do users request advice when distressed?
Knowledge questionWhy do users respond differently to advice versus reflective questions?
Wisdom questionWhen should an assistant advise, ask a reflective question, or remain silent?
Thinking toolsChallenge assumption: Is immediate advice always helpful? Systems: How do stress, trust, and autonomy interact? Inversion: When would silence cause harm?
Novelty / related workCompare the closest real papers on AI advice, reflective feedback, and user autonomy; do not assume the gap exists.
Method and analysisCompare advice / reflection / silence; ablate personalization; categorize harmful or unhelpful responses; test across situations; interview participants.
Possible conclusion“Reflection helped in some contexts but was frustrating in urgent situations”—only if supported by evidence.
Final workshop · 60 minutes

Your turn: one domain, one crazy thought

This is a planning exercise, not a literature review or full experiment. Write short answers; work individually or in pairs.

TimeDo this
0–10 minChoose one domain (mental health, habits, emotion regulation, simulated humans, etc.) and write one wild “What if…?” idea.
10–20 minWrite an information question (what?), a knowledge question (why/how?), and a wisdom question (when/what should we do?).
20–30 minUse two thinking tools to change or sharpen your question. Identify one possible student trap.
30–40 minGuess the closest prior work and a possible gap. Mark it unverified until you read real papers. Name your intended contribution.
40–50 minOutline one comparison, one ablation, one error analysis, and one robustness check (or explain why one does not fit).
50–60 minWrite a possible conclusion and one limitation. Share with a partner and revise one answer.
Submit one page: Domain + crazy thought; three levels of questions; two thinking tools; possible gap/contribution; evidence plan; possible conclusion and limitation. No coding or paper search required today.
