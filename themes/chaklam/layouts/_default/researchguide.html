From Research Idea to Top-Tier Publication

A practical research handbook for AIT Brain Lab: ask better questions, find genuine novelty, design credible evidence, and communicate contributions that matter.

Our ambition: CHI · ACL · NeurIPS · CVPR

Why top-tier research?

Before you read anything, please read my PDF talking about top-tier research. and our orientation slides (Canva)

Our goal is not simply to build AI applications. We aim to produce rigorous, original research that advances what the community knows about people, language, learning, and intelligent systems. A strong paper identifies an important question, establishes what is missing from prior work, offers a meaningful contribution, and supports its claims with convincing evidence.

1. Choosing topics

Start by exploring our Projects to identify a problem you genuinely want to solve. Then read recent papers from your target conference and ask: What is already known? What is still missing? Why does that gap matter? What could we learn that would generalize beyond one application?

Before implementation, prepare a one-page research pitch with the problem, research gap, research question, proposed contribution, closest related work, and evaluation plan. A useful starting question is: If my system works, what new knowledge will the research community gain?

2. Graduation criteria

Our current AIT Brain Lab expectations are:

  • Research Study: Develop a product or model justifying 6 months effort
  • Master Thesis: Submit 1 publication or participate on research grant that justify 1 year effort
  • Ph.D. Thesis: Submit 3 top-tier publications

3. Publication venue

Everyone in the lab should aim to publish. First read my blog on publication venues. We encourage submissions to strong conferences including ACM CHI (human-computer interaction), ACL (natural language processing), NeurIPS (machine learning), and CVPR (computer vision). Journals, including Neurocomputing, may also be appropriate depending on the work.

  • CHI: Make a contribution to how people interact with technology. Ground the work in HCI literature and use appropriate user studies, design inquiry, or empirical evidence. A polished interface alone is not enough.
  • ACL: Advance understanding of language technologies through a clear NLP research question, meaningful comparisons, careful error analysis, and evaluation suited to the claim. Using an LLM API alone is not enough.
  • NeurIPS: Contribute a substantive machine-learning method, insight, theory, or empirical finding. Test against strong baselines, investigate why it works, and examine limitations and robustness. A small improvement without insight is rarely compelling.
  • CVPR: For vision-focused research, demonstrate a clear computer-vision contribution and evaluate on suitable benchmarks and real-world conditions.

Choose the venue early. Read recent accepted papers and the current call for papers. Different venues value different questions and kinds of evidence; no checklist guarantees acceptance.

4. From an idea to a publishable paper

  1. Find the gap: Compare closely related papers and identify a specific unanswered question or limitation, not merely an unexplored combination of tools.
  2. State the contribution: Explain in one or two sentences what is new and why it matters. This might be a design insight, empirical finding, dataset, method, benchmark, or theoretical contribution.
  3. Design the evidence: Select baselines, measures, datasets, participants, and analyses that directly test your claims. Plan ablations for technical methods and appropriate controls for human studies.
  4. Run a pilot early: Test feasibility and revise weak assumptions before investing months in a full system.
  5. Analyze beyond headline scores: Examine failure cases, uncertainty, trade-offs, biases, generalizability, and alternative explanations.
  6. Write throughout: Keep your related-work matrix, methods, figures, and research narrative updated. Ask what a skeptical reviewer would challenge.

Publication readiness check: Can you explain the gap, novelty, evidence, strongest baseline, main limitations, and the takeaway for other researchers? If not, refine the research before focusing on paper formatting.

5. Ideation: where do good research ideas come from?

Start with a problem, surprising observation, disagreement in the literature, or limitation of an existing method—not with a tool you want to use. In our orientation, we recommend reading 10–20 relevant top-tier papers and discussing a mentor's initial topic until you can sharpen your own research question.

1. Find a contradiction

Two papers reach different conclusions. Which conditions, populations, or assumptions explain the difference?

2. Find a blind spot

What important behavior, failure case, language, user group, or deployment condition was excluded?

3. Challenge an assumption

What does the field take for granted? Test whether it still holds for modern LLMs or human–AI interaction.

4. Transfer carefully

Can a theory or method from another field explain a problem better? A new combination alone is not enough.

How do I know my idea is novel?

Novelty is a comparative claim, not a feeling. Search for the three to five closest papers, identify exactly what each contributes, and write: “Unlike A and B, we investigate / introduce / demonstrate X, which matters because Y.” Check both conceptual novelty and whether the contribution is meaningful. Finding no exact keyword match does not prove novelty.

Idea checkpoint: Write one sentence each for the problem, gap, research question, proposed contribution, and strongest evidence. If the gap is only “no one has used GPT-X for this application,” keep thinking.

6. Related work: how to find and read papers

Search deliberately. Begin with recent accepted papers from CHI, ACL/NAACL/EMNLP, NeurIPS/ICLR, or CVPR/ICCV/ECCV as relevant. Use Google Scholar, ACL Anthology, ACM Digital Library, and OpenReview. Follow references backward and citations forward. Preprints are useful discovery sources, but verify whether findings were peer reviewed and compare them critically.

Read in three passes: first title, abstract, figures, and conclusion; then research question, contribution, methods, and results; finally deep-read the closest papers, inspect their appendices/code, and reproduce key details if possible. Use directed learning: read with a question in mind rather than collecting PDFs indefinitely.

7. Methodology: design the study before building

Choose methods based on the claim you want to make. Specify the research question (RQ), hypotheses where appropriate, units of analysis, population or datasets, sampling, variables, comparison conditions, measures, analysis plan, and threats to validity.

Construct validity

Does your metric or questionnaire actually measure what you claim—trust, empathy, reasoning, robustness, or quality?

Internal validity

Could a confound, data leakage, ordering effect, or uncontrolled prompt explain the result?

External validity

Will findings transfer to other users, datasets, languages, tasks, or contexts?

Ethics & reproducibility

Plan consent, privacy, risk mitigation, data permissions, code/data release constraints, and documentation.

For CHI: articulate an HCI question, relevant theory, participant rationale, study protocol, and qualitative/quantitative methods. For ACL: specify tasks, corpora, splits, annotation quality, model/prompt settings, and linguistic phenomena. For NeurIPS: specify learning assumptions, algorithms, compute, strong baselines, robustness, and reproducibility. Use theory only when it genuinely helps explain or predict an outcome.

8. Experiments: what makes an evaluation convincing?

Every experiment should answer a different research question, not merely produce another table. A useful portfolio may include a main comparison, mechanism/ablation, generalization or robustness, and human-centered or qualitative validation—but the number is driven by the claims, not a mandatory quota.

  • Fair comparisons: same datasets/splits, equivalent access to information, justified compute budgets, and tuned competitive baselines.
  • Reliable measurements: preregistration when appropriate, sample-size or power rationale where applicable, confidence intervals, multiple seeds, and uncertainty estimates.
  • Human studies: randomization/counterbalancing where suitable, task design, recruitment, ethical review, manipulation checks, and participant safety.
  • Qualitative work: transparent sampling, coding or analysis approach, reflexivity, illustrative evidence, and a defensible connection from observations to claims.

Separate observation (what happened), interpretation (why it may have happened), and implication (what others should learn). A non-significant result is not proof of no effect; inspect uncertainty and study power. Do not hide negative findings or cherry-pick metrics.

10. What counts as a research contribution?

Our original orientation highlights four routes: a novel model, a novel application, new knowledge, and a novel methodology. Here is a broader taxonomy to help you articulate a paper's central claim.

1. New method / model

An algorithm, architecture, objective, or training procedure with demonstrated value.

2. New empirical knowledge

A surprising or generalizable finding about models, language, or human behavior.

3. New theory / framework

A conceptual explanation or predictive model that clarifies a phenomenon.

4. New design / system

An interaction technique or system that generates transferable design knowledge, not merely a demo.

5. New dataset / benchmark

A well-justified resource or evaluation protocol that unlocks meaningful new research.

6. New evaluation method

A more valid, reliable, or useful way to measure a research construct.

7. New application insight

A challenging application that reveals a broader method, limitation, or principle.

8. Replication / critical reassessment

A rigorous replication or challenge to a consequential established claim.

Remember: Technical difficulty is not the same as novelty. A contribution can be simple but insightful. You do not need all eight types—one strong central contribution, supported by evidence, is better than many weak claims.

11. Common reasons papers are rejected

  1. Unclear novelty: reviewers cannot distinguish the work from its closest predecessors.
  2. Weak significance: the problem or contribution is too narrow to matter beyond the case study.
  3. Mismatch with venue: a system demo is presented as an HCI insight, or an application is presented as a new ML method.
  4. Weak baselines / unfair comparisons: results do not isolate the claimed advance.
  5. Methodological flaws: confounds, leakage, insufficient data, unsuitable metrics, weak qualitative rigor, or unsupported causal claims.
  6. Missing analyses: no ablations, error analysis, robustness tests, or interpretation of limitations.
  7. Overclaiming: conclusions exceed the evidence or ignore uncertainty.
  8. Weak writing and positioning: the reader cannot follow the problem → gap → approach → evidence → contribution story.
  9. Ethical or reproducibility gaps: unclear data provenance, participant protections, code details, or computational reporting.

Before submission, ask a colleague to play the skeptical reviewer: What is new? Why care? Is the evaluation fair? What alternative explanation remains? What evidence would change your conclusion?

12. Common student problems—and how to think differently

“I cannot find a novel idea.”

Stop brainstorming in isolation. Compare the closest papers, reproduce a key result, inspect their limitations, and identify a contradiction or missing condition. Bring two candidate gaps to your mentor, not ten vague topics.

“I keep reading and never start.”

Switch from exploratory to directed learning. Set a specific research question, read only papers that resolve it, and create a one-page comparison matrix. Run a small pilot this week.

“I have built the app. Is that enough?”

No. Ask what claim the app allows you to test and what knowledge remains useful even if the app is never deployed. A demo is apparatus; a paper requires an insight and evidence.

“My model improves accuracy only slightly.”

Check variance, leakage, strong baselines, compute costs, and whether the gain is meaningful. Look for a mechanism, robustness advantage, or new failure-mode insight. Do not manufacture a story around noise.

“My experiment did not support the hypothesis.”

Investigate measurement quality, power, assumptions, and alternative explanations. A well-designed negative result can still teach something, but a failed implementation is not automatically a publishable finding.

“My topic is too big, and I am overwhelmed.”

Pick one RQ, one central contribution, one decisive experiment, and a short milestone. Separate essential evidence from optional features. Review scope with your mentor.

“I am stuck and do not want to bother my mentor.”

Send a concise update: what you tried, evidence collected, exact blocker, options considered, and the decision you need. Bring proposals, not only problems. Consistent progress beats disappearing for weeks.

“My mentor gave me a topic. Should I follow it exactly?”

Use the topic as a starting point, not a substitute for thinking. Read the literature, question assumptions, propose alternatives, and discuss evidence respectfully. Independence is not ignoring advice.

“I just want to graduate.”

Graduation and publication are related but distinct goals. Our lab encourages ambitious publication-focused work; align the project scope and timeline with the official AIT requirements and your mentor.

13. Writing, feedback, and submission

Write from the beginning, not after the experiments. A clear paper usually tells this story: background → important gap → research question → approach → evidence → contribution → limitations. Start with figures and tables that explain your main claim. Ensure every conclusion maps to a result.

  • Introduction: motivate the problem, identify the precise gap, state the approach, and summarize contributions without exaggeration.
  • Related work: synthesize, contrast, and position; do not merely enumerate citations.
  • Methods: provide enough detail to evaluate validity and support replication.
  • Results and discussion: report what happened, interpret it cautiously, explain implications and limitations.
  • Before submission: check the current venue's formatting, anonymization, ethics, reproducibility, and AI-use policies; arrange internal reviews early.
  • After reviews: sort comments into validity, novelty, clarity, and scope. Respond with evidence and precise revisions; distinguish misunderstandings from real flaws.

Orientation exercise: the one-page research pitch

Bring to your first research discussion: (1) a one-sentence problem, (2) three closest papers, (3) the gap, (4) two or three RQs, (5) one contribution claim, (6) a feasible pilot, (7) an evaluation plan, and (8) your biggest uncertainty. Our orientation suggests beginning with 10–20 strong papers and refining the pitch through mentor discussions.

Lab working habits

Use the weekly meeting to show evidence, not only activity. Keep code and experiments reproducible, share progress proactively, learn from senior researchers, and prepare proposal materials in advance. Useful lab resources include our Python project starter and GitHub project board. Confirm current lab logistics and equipment access with your mentor because these change over time.
