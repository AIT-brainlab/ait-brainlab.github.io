Ultimate Guide to Your Research Success

By Chaklam

Research is not just building an AI app. It is asking a worthwhile question, gathering convincing evidence, and helping others understand something new.

Think deeply→Find a meaningful question→Test it rigorously→Share useful insight
The research test: If someone reads your paper but never uses your application, what valuable knowledge will they gain?

What top-tier research looks like (PDF)

WHY RESEARCH IS HARD

From information to wisdom: Bloom’s pyramid

A research question can grow from describing what happens, to explaining why, to judging what should be done. Bloom’s pyramid illustrates increasingly demanding ways of thinking.

Create · wisdom-informedDesign an AI feedback strategy; devise a new method for validating simulated humans.
Evaluate · judgmentDecide when AI advice is appropriate; assess whether simulated participants are credible.
Analyze · knowledgeExplain which users reject advice; identify where simulations diverge from real behavior.
Apply · using knowledgeBuild a feedback prototype; run a simulated-human study.
Understand · informationExplain emotion-regulation theory; explain how a persona simulator works.
Remember · informationRecall emotion-regulation terms; list simulated-human evaluation metrics.
Two question journeys: Emotion regulation: “How often does AI help?” → “Why does it help some users?” → “When should AI intervene or remain silent?” Simulated humans: “How similar are outputs?” → “Where do simulated people diverge from real people?” → “When is simulation appropriate for research decisions?”

Thinking tools help us climb toward explanation and judgment by challenging assumptions and exploring causes. Bloom and information–knowledge–wisdom are distinct frameworks; this mapping is a teaching analogy, not a scientific equivalence.

THINK MORE DEEPLY

Thinking tools

ToolTry this immediately
Challenge assumptions“Personalization helps everyone.” What if too much personalization reduces autonomy?
First principlesWhat actually causes lasting habit change, rather than assuming reminders are the answer?
Systems thinkingHow do stress, motivation, social pressure, reminders, and habit success reinforce one another?
Second-order thinkingA nudge improves action today. Could it create dependence or fatigue after a month?
InversionHow could an AI emotion-regulation tool make a user feel worse?
Compare alternativesWould reflective questions work better than direct advice, and for whom?

Try one tool on your own idea. You are not memorizing definitions—you are generating better research questions.

SECTION

Novelty

A good question must be new to the research community, interesting, and important—not just new to you.

NOVELTY

Novelty: new, interesting, and important

Weak novelty claimPotentially stronger claimWhy it might matter
“First AI habit app at my university.”Identify when autonomy-supportive nudges work better than controlling nudges.Generalizable insight about behavior and design.
“First LLM for emotion regulation.”Find when reflective questions help but directive advice backfires.Explains a meaningful interaction trade-off.
“More simulated personas.”Establish when simulated humans fail to reproduce real human disagreement.Improves research validity.
“New prompt for mental-health chat.”Show a reproducible safety–helpfulness trade-off across situations.Explains a consequential limit.
“A new dashboard.”Test whether editable explanations change users’ ability to reject bad AI advice.Produces knowledge about interaction design.
Novelty is judged by other researchers. Ask: “Compared with the closest papers, is this genuinely different? Would CHI/ACL reviewers find it interesting? Does the difference matter?” Being first is insufficient.

How many research areas overlap?

Example: AI nudges for emotion regulation overlaps (1) emotion-regulation psychology, (2) digital behavior-change and nudges, (3) human–AI interaction, and (4) LLM safety/personalization. Search the closest work in each area and especially at their intersections. A gap in one area may already have been studied in another.

10 · Contributions

What kind of contribution are you making?

Method or model

A new algorithm, architecture, objective, or training procedure that shows demonstrated value.

Empirical knowledge

A robust, non-obvious finding about people, language, or models.

Theory or framework

An explanation or predictive account that clarifies a phenomenon.

Design or system

An interaction technique that produces transferable design knowledge—not just a demo.

Dataset or benchmark

A resource that enables meaningful new research or exposes a neglected capability.

Evaluation method

A more valid, reliable, or useful way to measure something important.

Application insight

A demanding real-world setting that reveals a broader principle or limitation.

Replication or reassessment

A careful test that confirms, qualifies, or challenges an important claim.

Focus: One clear central contribution is usually stronger than a long list of loosely connected claims. Technical difficulty is not the same as research value.
Avoidable mistakes

Common traps of students

Starting with a tool

“I want to use an LLM” without a meaningful question.

Calling implementation novelty

“Nobody built this exact app” without comparing the closest research.

Shopping-list literature review

Summarizing papers one by one without identifying agreement, contradictions, or gaps.

Weak evidence

Claiming a habit app changes behavior after only measuring whether users liked it.

Only reporting averages

Ignoring error analysis, subgroups, negative cases, and robustness.

Overclaiming

“Simulated humans behave like real humans” after testing only a few tasks.

Related work

Connect closely related findings into an argument that leads to your question.

RELATED WORK

Related work is synthesis—not a shopping list

A shopping list says “Paper A did X; Paper B did Y.” Synthesis asks what the studies collectively establish, where they disagree, and what is still unknown.

Example topic: AI nudges for habit formation and emotion regulation. The studies below are illustrative hypothetical examples, not real citations.

Closest studyWhat it testsWhat it suggestsWhat it leaves open
A · Reminder nudgesDaily reminders vs no reminders for habit adherenceReminders can increase short-term actionWhat happens to autonomy and long-term engagement?
B · Personalized nudgesPersonalized vs generic remindersPersonalization can increase engagementDoes it become intrusive for some people?
C · Autonomy-supportive nudgesChoice-based vs directive remindersPerceived autonomy may improveDoes autonomy matter when the nudge comes from an AI?
Your studyAI personalized nudges × autonomy-supportive vs directive wordingTest adherence, autonomy, and long-term disengagementFind when personalization helps or backfires
Synthesis: A, B, and C all study nudges but vary the mechanism and outcome. Together they suggest a gap: we do not yet know how AI personalization and autonomy-supportive language interact. Verify this gap using actual papers before claiming novelty.

Use a comparison matrix with research question, theory, participants/data, IV, DV, method, results, limitations. Look for patterns and disagreements—not merely a summary per paper.

Methodology

A strong question needs a study designed to answer it convincingly.

METHODOLOGY & EVIDENCE

What makes a good methodology?

Example: Does autonomy-supportive AI nudging improve habit formation without undermining autonomy?

Define before collecting dataConcrete example
Independent variable (IV)Nudge condition: autonomy-supportive, directive, or no nudge.
Dependent variables (DV)Habit adherence, perceived autonomy, disengagement, and possibly mood.
ConfoundersBaseline motivation, prior habit strength, stress, task difficulty, prior AI use.
Controls / comparisonSame app, schedule, tasks, and exposure where possible; appropriate baseline.
Randomization / assignmentRandomly assign conditions when feasible; counterbalance within-subject order if needed.
Participants / datasetsDefine eligibility, recruitment, sample-size rationale, dataset provenance and splits.
Tasks and proceduresState exactly what participants do, when nudges occur, duration, consent, and exclusions.
Planned analysisSpecify primary comparisons, effect sizes/uncertainty, error or qualitative analysis, and subgroup checks.
ReproducibilityDocument prompts, models, versions, materials, analysis code, and deviations.
Venue fitCHI: theory, design, human outcomes and qualitative depth. ACL: language behavior, baselines, linguistic/error analyses and reproducible evaluation.

What evidence will persuade a reviewer?

AnalysisQuestion it answers
Baseline comparisonDoes the method beat a fair alternative?
Ablation studyWhich component actually causes the improvement?
Error analysisWhere and why does the method fail?
Linguistic analysis (ACL)Which language phenomena—negation, sarcasm, pragmatics—drive outcomes?
Robustness / generalizationDoes the finding hold across tasks, populations, prompts, or datasets?
Human / qualitative analysis (CHI)How do people experience the system, and what mechanisms explain behavior?
Uncertainty and alternative explanationsHow strong is the evidence and what else could explain it?
Not every study needs every analysis. Choose analyses that test your claims and can reveal why a result happened. Predefine primary outcomes; report limitations honestly.

12 · Your next step

Prepare a one-page research pitch.

Bring a concrete argument to your supervisor—not just a topic or a list of ideas.

  1. Problem: What important situation or challenge are you studying?
  2. Closest work: What are the three most relevant papers, and what did they establish?
  3. Gap: What remains unanswered, and why does it matter?
  4. Research question: What exactly will you investigate?
  5. Contribution: What new knowledge or capability could result?
  6. Evidence: What baseline, measure, dataset/participants, and analysis would convince a skeptical reviewer?
  7. Risk: What is the biggest uncertainty, and what cheap pilot can test it?
Final reviewer test
What is new? Why should anyone care? Is the comparison fair? What did you learn from failures? Why do the results happen? When might the claim stop holding?

WORKSHOP

Your 60-minute research idea workshop

Work alone or in pairs. No coding or paper search is required during this hour. Your novelty claim is a hypothesis to verify later.

TimeYour taskWrite down
0–8 minChoose a domain and one crazy thought.“Could AI reduce human creativity?”
8–18 minWrite an information question, a knowledge question, and a wisdom question.What? Why? When/should we?
18–28 minApply two thinking tools (challenge assumptions, first principles, systems, second order, inversion).How did your question change?
28–38 minSketch overlapping research areas and what novelty would need checking.2–4 areas; 2 closest-paper questions to search later.
38–50 minDesign the study: IV, DV, confounders, controls, participants, tasks, procedures.One defensible comparison.
50–57 minPlan baseline, ablation, error, robustness, and/or qualitative analyses as relevant.Which analysis would explain why?
57–60 minWrite three potential “wow” conclusions—conditional, not invented findings.What could the world learn if evidence supports it?
One-page output: domain + crazy thought; three levels of questions; two thinking tools; novelty intersections; IV/DV/confounders/procedure; analysis plan; three possible insights. Use the following two slides as worked examples.
WORKSHOP EXAMPLE

Worked example 1: Could AI reduce human creativity?

Domain: Human–AI interaction and creativity. Crazy thought: “What if AI makes people less creative?”

StepWorked answer
Information questionDo people using an AI ideation assistant produce fewer distinct ideas than people without it?
Knowledge questionDoes early AI suggestion exposure anchor people on similar ideas and reduce diversity?
Wisdom questionWhen should an AI assistant delay suggestions or encourage independent ideation?
Thinking toolsChallenge assumptions: more ideas ≠ more originality. Systems: suggestions ↔ confidence ↔ exploration. Second order: long-term reliance.
Research overlapsCreativity research + cognitive fixation + HCI co-creation + generative AI.
IV / DVIV: no AI vs immediate AI vs delayed AI. DV: originality, diversity, usefulness, self-efficacy.
Confounders / controlsPrior creativity, domain expertise, task difficulty; randomized assignment, matched tasks/time.
ProcedureParticipants brainstorm, optionally receive AI suggestions, then independently generate ideas; blind human ratings.
AnalysesBaseline comparisons; ablation of timing; idea-overlap/error taxonomy; robustness across tasks; interviews on fixation.

Three possible “wow” insights to investigate

  1. AI may increase idea quantity while reducing diversity.
  2. Delayed AI assistance may preserve originality better than immediate assistance.
  3. AI could help novices but constrain experts—or vice versa—revealing who benefits and when.

These are hypotheses, not established findings.

WORKSHOP EXAMPLE

Worked example 2: RL for mental-health support

Domain: Mental health, emotion regulation, reinforcement learning (RL). Crazy thought: “Could an AI learn the right moment to offer emotion-regulation support?”

StepWorked answer
Information questionWhich supportive prompts do users engage with, and when?
Knowledge questionHow do timing, context, and feedback type affect short-term engagement and well-being?
Wisdom questionWhen should the system intervene, abstain, or recommend human support, considering safety and autonomy?
Thinking toolsFirst principles: what is the actual reward? Inversion: how could optimizing engagement cause harm? Systems: feedback ↔ mood ↔ reliance.
Research overlapsEmotion-regulation theory + just-in-time interventions + RL/bandits + HAI + mental-health safety.
IV / DVIV: fixed policy vs contextual bandit vs user-controlled timing (where ethically feasible). DV: acceptability, appropriate engagement, well-being proxies, burden.
Confounders / controlsBaseline symptoms, stress, prior therapy/app use; safe eligibility criteria, controlled timing, expert oversight.
ProcedureStart with offline/simulated policy evaluation and safety review; only then consider a consented, supervised low-risk human pilot.
AnalysesBaseline policy comparisons; ablate context features; error/safety cases; robustness across user groups; qualitative burden and autonomy.

Three possible “wow” insights to investigate

  1. Maximizing engagement might not maximize well-being.
  2. Learning when not to intervene may be more important than choosing the message.
  3. A personalized policy may help one group while overburdening another, requiring explicit safeguards.

These are hypotheses, not results. Avoid unsafe online exploration or clinical claims without appropriate ethics and clinical oversight.
