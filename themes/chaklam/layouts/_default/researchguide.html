Ultimate Guide to Your Research Success

From your first research idea to evidence you can defend.

By Chaklam

01 · Start with purpose

What is research really for?

You do not need to invent a whole new field. You need a meaningful question and trustworthy evidence.

Building

“I created a medical chatbot.” Useful engineering, but what have we learned?

Measuring

“My chatbot scored 90%.” Better—but a number alone explains little.

Researching

“It fails on negation, and grounding reduces this failure under specific conditions.” Now others can learn from the result.

Ask yourself: If my software disappeared, what would my paper still teach?
02 · From results to judgment

Information → Knowledge → Wisdom

Imagine you tested a medical visual question-answering model.

Information · What happened?

It answered 90 of 100 questions correctly.

Knowledge · Why and when?

After analyzing errors, you find that negation questions are disproportionately difficult.

Wisdom · What should we do?

Use the model cautiously on those questions, show uncertainty, and require human review where consequences are serious.

Numbers are useful. Understanding why makes them meaningful. Good judgment considers consequences and limitations.
03 · Depth of thinking

Bloom’s Taxonomy: go beyond “I built it”

Bloom describes kinds of cognitive work. It is not identical to information → knowledge → wisdom, but the two ideas help explain deeper research thinking.

1 · Remember

Define RAG.

2 · Understand

Explain why retrieval may help an LLM.

3 · Apply

Build a working RAG pipeline.

4 · Analyze

Find which questions and documents cause failures.

5 · Evaluate

Judge whether RAG helps compared with a fair baseline.

6 · Create

Propose and test a method that addresses a well-defined gap.

A master’s thesis usually needs more than Apply. Look for opportunities to Analyze, Evaluate, and Create credible knowledge.
04 · Tools for better questions

Thinking tools are not theories to memorize

Use them when you are stuck, overly confident, or unsure what to investigate.

First principles

What is the actual problem, without assuming a particular technology?

Systems thinking

What else changes when we improve one part?

Second-order thinking

What happens after the immediate result?

Thinking in bets

How confident am I—and what evidence would change my mind?

Critical thinking

What alternative explanation fits these results?

Inversion

How could this idea or experiment fail?

You do not need to use all six. Choose one question that helps you think better today.
05 · First principles

Don’t begin with your favorite model

A student says: “I want to use a larger LLM for medical VQA.”

Step 1 · Name the real problem

Answers are unreliable for some clinical images.

Step 2 · Identify what must work

Image interpretation, question understanding, and safe uncertainty handling.

Step 3 · Challenge assumptions

Would a larger model solve image grounding? What evidence says so?

Step 4 · Find a research question

Which source of error dominates, and which intervention addresses it?

The research idea comes from the problem, not the model name.
06 · Systems and consequences

Better technology can create new problems

Suppose an AI assistant becomes more accurate.

Higher accuracy→More user trust→Less checking→Missed errors

First-order effect

Users get more correct answers.

Second-order effect

Users may rely on the system more, even when it is wrong.

Systems-thinking question

How do accuracy, confidence, explanations, user expertise, and verification interact?

A good HCI research question: When does improved AI performance lead to better human decisions—and when does it not?
07 · Think in bets

Treat your idea as a hypothesis, not your identity

“I think my interface will help users detect AI mistakes.” Good start. But how sure are you?

State your belief

“I expect the interface to improve error detection.”

Look for disconfirming evidence

Maybe it makes users feel confident without improving decisions.

Run a small, fair test

Compare detection of incorrect AI advice with and without the interface.

Update your belief

If the result is negative, revise the explanation or abandon the idea.

Strong researchers are willing to be wrong. What evidence would change your mind?
08 · Bridge to ideation

Now: where do research ideas come from?

You have learned to look beneath a result, question assumptions, and consider consequences.

Notice

What is failing, confusing, surprising, or important?

Question

Why does it happen? What assumption might be wrong?

Investigate

What could I compare or measure to find out?

Research idea starter: “I noticed ___. I wonder why ___. I could investigate it by ___.”
01 · Start here

Research is not just building something.

A research paper should help other people understand something they did not understand before.

Important problem→Clear question→Convincing evidence→New knowledge

Build

Make a chatbot, model, tool, or interface.

Research

Find out what it teaches us, why it works, when it fails, or how it changes people’s experience.

The test: If the application disappeared tomorrow, what would the paper still teach the research community?

02 · Idea generation

Where do good ideas come from?

Research ideas often begin with curiosity, not technology. Here are nine useful starting points.

1. A real problem

Doctors struggle to verify evidence behind a medical VQA answer. What evidence display helps?

2. A surprising failure

A small model is accurate overall but fails on negation. Why?

3. Two conflicting findings

AI advice helps some people but causes over-reliance in others. What explains this?

4. An overlooked group or context

What happens when a human–AI interface is used by novice rather than expert users?

5. A questionable assumption

Does showing more explanations always improve trust calibration?

6. A theory from another field

Can cognitive-load theory predict when an AI interface becomes overwhelming?

7. A new technical capability

Can smaller models support a previously impractical on-device workflow? What new trade-off appears?

8. A missing measurement

Accuracy hides unsafe medical reasoning. What evaluation captures clinically consequential errors?

9. Reproduce and challenge

Does a published simulated-human finding hold for different personas, tasks, or real participants?

Turn an observation into research: I noticed ___ → I wonder why ___ → Existing studies do not explain ___ → I could test ___.

04 · Novelty check

“New to me” is not the same as novel research.

Compare your question and contribution—not just your model name, dataset, or interface.

Weak novelty argumentStronger possibilityWhy?
First medical VQA app in our hospitalDiscover a previously unmeasured class of clinically consequential visual grounding failuresPotential generalizable insight, not just location.
Use a newer small LLMExplain a reproducible accuracy–latency–calibration trade-off across model sizesIdentifies conditions and mechanisms.

More synthetic participantsDevelop and validate a method to detect when simulations diverge from human behaviorAddresses construct validity.

Find 3–5 closest papers→Compare their questions→Identify missing evidence→Explain the significance
Novelty sentence: “Prior work establishes ___. It remains unclear ___. We test ___, which matters because ___.” If prior work already answers it, refine your question.
10 · Contributions

What kind of contribution are you making?

Method or model

A new algorithm, architecture, objective, or training procedure that shows demonstrated value.

Empirical knowledge

A robust, non-obvious finding about people, language, or models.

Theory or framework

An explanation or predictive account that clarifies a phenomenon.

Design or system

An interaction technique that produces transferable design knowledge—not just a demo.

Dataset or benchmark

A resource that enables meaningful new research or exposes a neglected capability.

Evaluation method

A more valid, reliable, or useful way to measure something important.

Application insight

A demanding real-world setting that reveals a broader principle or limitation.

Replication or reassessment

A careful test that confirms, qualifies, or challenges an important claim.

Focus: One clear central contribution is usually stronger than a long list of loosely connected claims. Technical difficulty is not the same as research value.
11 · Rejection and student pitfalls

Common reasons papers are rejected

Unclear novelty or significance

Reviewers cannot see what is new, why it matters, or how it differs from the closest work.

Wrong venue fit

An application demo is presented as an HCI insight, or an API integration as a new ML method.

Weak evidence

Unfair baselines, unsuitable metrics, missing controls, leakage, or insufficient analysis.

Missing insight

Only headline scores are reported; no error analysis, robustness checks, or explanation of why results happen.

Overclaiming

Conclusions go beyond the evidence or ignore uncertainty and limitations.

Unclear reporting

Readers cannot understand or reproduce the procedure, or ethics and data provenance are unclear.

Common student traps

  • Reading forever: define a question, build a comparison matrix, then run a small pilot.
  • Building without a claim: state what the app lets you investigate and what others will learn.
  • Only a tiny score gain: check variance, baselines, cost, robustness, and mechanism—never invent a story around noise.
  • Getting stuck quietly: report what you tried, the evidence, the exact blocker, and the options you considered.
  • Scope too big: choose one research question, one central contribution, and one decisive experiment.

06 · Methodology

Bad methodology means your evidence cannot answer your question.

Example research question: Does an AI explanation help doctors detect incorrect medical VQA answers?

DecisionBad approachBetter approach
Who participates?Ask five friends who are not clinicians.Recruit appropriate clinical participants and justify sample size and scope.
What is compared?Everyone uses the explained AI only.Compare relevant explanation and control conditions.
What is measured?Ask “Did you like it?”Measure error detection, appropriate reliance, time, and perceived usefulness as distinct outcomes.
What can bias results?Easy questions in one condition; hard questions in another.Match or randomize questions and counterbalance order where appropriate.
What can you conclude?“Explanations make doctors safer.”State exactly which outcomes improved, under which conditions, with uncertainty and limitations.
Simple test: If the result is positive, could it be explained by easier tasks, different participants, extra information, or an unsuitable metric? If yes, fix the study design first.
07 · Methodology by venue

Choose methods that fit the claim.

VenueCore questionMethodological priorities
CHIWhat do we learn about people and interaction?Relevant HCI literature/theory, justified participants, credible study protocol, careful qualitative and/or quantitative analysis, design implications.
ACLWhat language capability or limitation is revealed?Clear task and corpora, valid data splits, annotation quality, competitive baselines, linguistic phenomena, reproducible model/prompt settings.
NeurIPSWhat method or general ML insight is advanced?Strong baselines, fair compute, ablations, uncertainty, multiple seeds where appropriate, robustness and reproducibility.
One rule: Design the experiment around the claim you want to make. Do not choose a convenient metric first and invent a claim afterward.
08 · Experiments

Do not run experiments just to fill tables.

Imagine your claim is: “Grounded medical VQA reduces unsafe answers.” Each experiment should test one part of that claim.

What students should askWhat to doWhat it teaches us
1. Is the new approach actually better?Compare against strong VQA and retrieval baselines on the same test set.Whether it improves the intended outcome.
2. What part makes it better?Remove retrieval, grounding, or verification separately.Which component drives the improvement.
3. Where does it still fail?Categorize errors: visual recognition, negation, hallucination, clinical reasoning.Failure mechanisms rather than only accuracy.
4. Does it work outside the easy setting?Test another dataset, question type, or image quality.Robustness and limits of generalization.
5. Does the improvement matter?Have qualified experts assess clinical consequence and usefulness.Whether benchmark gains have practical relevance.
Student exercise: Write your main claim in one sentence. Then ask: “What evidence would convince a skeptical reviewer that this is true?” Not every paper needs all five experiments; choose those that test your claims.
09 · Analysis

What reviewers look for beyond the headline score.

A strong paper explains not only whether a result happened, but also why, when, for whom, and at what cost.

Error analysis

Group failures into meaningful categories. Which errors dominate? Which are new? What causes them? Show representative examples and counts.

Linguistic analysis

For language work: examine negation, ambiguity, coreference, long-distance dependencies, pragmatics, morphology, code-switching, or other phenomena relevant to the claim.

Robustness analysis

Vary domains, languages, prompts, noise, populations, or assumptions. Identify which changes break the result and which do not.

Ablation and sensitivity

Remove components or vary key settings. Determine whether the proposed mechanism actually explains the gain.

Subgroups and individual differences

For CHI: how do experience, goals, ability, context, or interaction style change outcomes? Avoid unsupported subgroup claims.

Qualitative and behavioral insight

Use interviews, interaction traces, think-aloud data, or expert review to explain patterns that a score cannot reveal.

Uncertainty and significance

Report variability and uncertainty. Distinguish statistical significance from practical importance; do not treat “not significant” as proof of no effect.

Trade-offs, bias, and limitations

Who benefits or loses? What resources are required? Where is performance uneven? What alternative explanation remains?

Write every analysis as: Observation (what happened) → Interpretation (why it may have happened) → Implication (what researchers should learn or do next).
12 · Your next step

Prepare a one-page research pitch.

Bring a concrete argument to your supervisor—not just a topic or a list of ideas.

  1. Problem: What important situation or challenge are you studying?
  2. Closest work: What are the three most relevant papers, and what did they establish?
  3. Gap: What remains unanswered, and why does it matter?
  4. Research question: What exactly will you investigate?
  5. Contribution: What new knowledge or capability could result?
  6. Evidence: What baseline, measure, dataset/participants, and analysis would convince a skeptical reviewer?
  7. Risk: What is the biggest uncertainty, and what cheap pilot can test it?
Final reviewer test
What is new? Why should anyone care? Is the comparison fair? What did you learn from failures? Why do the results happen? When might the claim stop holding?

Final activity · 60 minutes

One idea, four sentences

Work alone or with a partner. No coding, paper search, or presentation required. You can finish this even if you do not have a thesis topic yet.

0–10 min · Pick a problem

Choose something you have seen, read about, or found confusing.

10–25 min · Ask a question

Make it narrower: what exactly do you want to understand?

25–40 min · Imagine a test

What would you compare, observe, or measure?

40–60 min · Discuss in pairs

Explain your idea; your partner asks one difficult question. Improve one sentence.

Only four sentences to submit:
1. My research question is…
2. This matters because…
3. My idea might be new because… (not yet verified)
4. I could test it by…
