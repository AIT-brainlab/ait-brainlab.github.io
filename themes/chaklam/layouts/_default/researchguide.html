Ground rule

Separate generation from judgment

Must the first idea already be novel?

No. Early judgment suppresses useful possibilities. Generate several questions first. Then challenge them, inspect the literature, and decide which one deserves a study.

Curiosity produces candidates. Evidence determines whether a candidate becomes research.
01
Part 1 · Idea generation

Where research ideas come from

Five starting moves, learned one at a time

Technique 1 · Observe a problem

Start with a problem you care about

What problem do people still face?

Look at what people do in real life. Who has the problem? When does it happen? Why does it matter?

1
Affective Diary · Lindström et al., CHI 2006

People often find it hard to understand their own emotions. The researchers built a diary that combines body data with personal notes and pictures.

2
Experimental evidence on the productivity effects of generative AI · Noy & Zhang, Science 2023

Many people spend a long time on writing tasks. The researchers tested whether ChatGPT could help them write faster and better.

3
EmpatheticDialogues · Rashkin et al., ACL 2019

Chatbots often give replies that do not match a person's feelings. The researchers created conversations that could help computers learn more caring replies.

Workshop · Observe

From friction to a question

Seven minutes
  1. List three frustrations from study, work, or daily life.
  2. Choose one. Name the affected person and setting.
  3. Ask why it persists.
  4. Write a question that does not assume the solution.
Weak

“I want to build an AI tutor.”

Strong

“When do students accept an AI explanation without checking whether they understood it?”

A clear problem can lead to many possible research ideas.
Technique 2 · Notice a surprise

Something unexpected happened

What did you expect, and what happened instead?

An unexpected result may show that we have missed something important. Write down what you expected. Then write what you actually saw.

1
Gorillas in our midst · Simons & Chabris, 1999

People watched a video and counted ball passes. Many did not see a person in a gorilla suit walk through the scene.

2
Gender Shades · Buolamwini & Gebru, 2018

Face-analysis systems worked much better for some groups than others. The largest errors affected darker-skinned women.

3
The weirdest people in the world? · Henrich et al., 2010

Many psychology studies used people from a small set of wealthy countries. Their behavior did not always represent people in the rest of the world.

Workshop · Surprise

The expectation gap

Complete four lines
  • I expected ______.
  • Instead, ______ happened.
  • One explanation might be ______.
  • I could distinguish explanations by measuring ______.
Weak

“The model performed badly. I will tune it.”

Strong

“Accuracy fell only for short, ambiguous requests. Does missing conversational context explain the drop?”

A surprising pattern becomes research when it creates competing explanations.
Technique 3 · Use a theory

A theory can help explain your problem

Is there an idea that helps explain why this problem happens?

Start with a problem you care about. Then find a theory that gives you a possible explanation. The theory should help you decide what to test.

1
Impact of User Mobility on Attentional Tunneling in Handheld AR · Parmar & Silpasuwanchai, CHI EA 2023

Problem: people using augmented reality while walking may miss things around them. An idea from aviation, called attentional tunneling, helped explain why.

2
Attention Regulation Framework · Niksirat et al., TOCHI 2019

Problem: meditation apps often tell everyone to follow the same audio guide. Ideas about relaxation and restoring attention helped the team design more flexible practice.

3
From Overload to Convergence · Parmar & Silpasuwanchai, CHI 2026

Problem: people become overloaded when negotiating many issues with AI. Bayesian reasoning helped show which agreements were becoming more likely.

Workshop · Theory

Start with a problem you care about

  1. Write the problem in one simple sentence.
  2. Ask why it may happen.
  3. Find one theory that gives a possible answer.
  4. Use the theory to predict what you will see.
Weak

“I want to use cognitive load theory to study AI.”

Strong

“Students may ignore long AI explanations because their working memory becomes full. I predict that beginners will learn more from shorter explanations with one example.”

Use a theory when it helps you explain the problem and plan a test.
Technique 4 · Learn from another field

Other fields may know something useful

Who else studies a problem like yours?

Start with your problem. Look for another field that studies a similar human behavior or technical challenge. Ask whether one of its ideas can help you see your problem differently.

1
Attentional tunneling in handheld AR

An aviation safety idea helped explain why people may miss real-world events while looking at augmented reality.

2
Bayesian visualization for negotiation

An idea from probability helped people understand how close they were to an agreement with AI.

3
Human-centered tools for imperfect algorithms · Kocielnik et al., CHI 2019

Ideas from human-computer interaction helped people understand when an algorithm may be wrong and what they could do next.

Workshop · Another field

Start with your problem

  1. Write a topic or problem you care about.
  2. Ask which other field studies a similar problem.
  3. Choose one useful idea from that field.
  4. Explain how the idea changes your research question.
Weak

“I will combine AI and psychology.”

Strong

“People sometimes reject advice that feels controlling. Psychology calls this reactance. Does forceful wording make users reject correct AI advice?”

The other field should help you ask a better question about your problem.
Technique 5 · Continue from a paper

The next question

What question could we ask after reading this paper?

Look at what the paper studied and what it did not study. Check the people, setting, task, length of time, and result. Then search newer papers to make sure the question is still open.

1
HCI: Attentional tunneling in handheld AR

The study found that walking made people slower to notice real-world events. Next question: which interface design can reduce this problem?

2
ACL: EmpatheticDialogues

The study helped chatbots give replies that sound more caring. Next question: which kind of caring reply works best for each person and situation?

3
EEG and BCI: the P300 speller · Farwell & Donchin, 1988

The system let people choose letters using brain signals. Next question: can prediction make writing faster without causing more mistakes or taking control away from the user?

Workshop · Continue from a paper

What could we study next?

  1. Write the paper's main finding in simple words.
  2. Write one thing the paper did not study.
  3. Explain why that missing part matters.
  4. Search for newer work before saying it is a research gap.
Weak

“Use a newer model on the same dataset.”

Strong

“The paper shows that AI helps people finish a task now. Will they still do well later when the AI is removed?”

A good next question helps us understand something new.
02
Part 1 · Critical thinking

Challenge one assumption at a time

Three tools for turning a broad question into an explanatory one

Technique 6 · First principles

Claims and foundations

What must be true for your claim to make sense?

Break the claim into definitions, assumptions, and necessary conditions. Test the weakest foundation first.

1
With Little Power Comes Great Responsibility · Card et al., EMNLP 2020

Power analysis challenged whether common NLP experiments could detect meaningful improvements.

2
Datasheets for Datasets · Gebru et al., 2018

Dataset claims depend on provenance, composition, intended uses, and limitations.

3
Model Cards for Model Reporting · Mitchell et al., 2019

Model performance requires explicit context, subgroups, and use conditions.

Workshop · First principles

Assumption audit

Claim: “Personalized AI feedback improves learning.”

  1. Define personalized.
  2. Define learning.
  3. Name three necessary assumptions.
  4. Choose the most fragile assumption.
Weak

“Personalization is usually helpful.”

Strong

“The feedback must diagnose the learner correctly. If the diagnosis is wrong, personalization may reinforce the misconception.”

The strongest experiment often targets the most fragile assumption.
Technique 7 · Alternative explanations

Rival causal stories

What else could have produced the same pattern?

List plausible confounds, selection effects, measurement artifacts, and reverse causality before choosing a method.

1
Using big data to emulate a target trial · Hernán & Robins, 2016

The target-trial framework makes causal assumptions and time zero explicit.

2
The spread of true and false news online · Vosoughi et al., 2018

Diffusion differences required separating human behavior from automated activity.

3
Facebook use predicts declines in subjective well-being · Kross et al., 2013

Experience sampling and longitudinal measures probed competing explanations for association.

Workshop · Alternatives

Three explanations, one discriminator

Observation: frequent AI-tool users finish assignments faster.

  1. Write three explanations.
  2. For each, predict another observable pattern.
  3. Choose one measure or comparison that separates them.
Weak

“AI causes productivity.”

Strong

“Users may be more skilled, may choose easier tasks, or may gain speed from AI. Random assignment or within-person crossover helps separate these stories.”

Good methods emerge from the rival explanation you need to rule out.
Technique 8 · Second-order and systems thinking

Effects after the first effect

If the intervention works, what changes next?

Trace adaptation, incentives, feedback loops, spillovers, and delayed harm. Decide where the system boundary lies.

1
Inherent trade-offs in the fair determination of risk scores · Kleinberg et al., 2016

Different fairness criteria can become mutually incompatible when base rates differ.

2
Delayed Impact of Fair Machine Learning · Liu et al., ICML 2018

Immediate fairness interventions can produce different long-run outcomes.

3
Runaway Feedback Loops in Predictive Policing · Ensign et al., 2017

Biased observations can reinforce future deployments and data collection.

Workshop · Systems

The consequence map

Intervention: an AI assistant answers students immediately.

  1. Write the immediate benefit.
  2. Trace a behavior change after one month.
  3. Name one feedback loop.
  4. Add a delayed outcome to measure.
Weak

“Students complete more tasks.”

Strong

“Completion may rise while help-seeking becomes habitual. Measure unaided transfer after access ends.”

Measure what the intervention changes in the system, not only the first outcome.
Session checkpoint

The question should now contain tension

BeforeAfter critical thinking
Does AI feedback improve learning?When does immediate AI feedback improve task completion but weaken unaided transfer?
Can we make chatbots empathetic?Which response strategy helps users feel understood, and when does it become intrusive?
A useful research question allows a plausible, informative “no.”

Part 1 · Literature synthesis and novelty

Turn papers into a map of knowledge

The goal is an argument, not an annotated bibliography

Technique 9 · Synthesis matrix

Read across papers

Where do studies agree, differ, and leave uncertainty?

Create columns for question, theory, population, method, comparison, outcome, finding, and limitation. Compare by column before writing prose.

1
PRISMA 2020 statement · Page et al., 2021

Transparent search, selection, and reporting make evidence synthesis reproducible.

2
Analyzing the Past to Prepare for the Future · Webster & Watson, 2002

A concept-centric review organizes ideas across authors rather than paper by paper.

3
Measuring inconsistency in meta-analyses · Higgins et al., BMJ 2003

Heterogeneity helps distinguish an average pattern from its boundary conditions.

Workshop · Synthesis

Three papers, one paragraph

PaperMain contributionWhat remains
Rashkin et al.Dataset and benchmark for empathetic dialogueWhich response intentions help?
Lin et al.Mixture of listeners conditioned on emotionHow should intentions be represented?
Welivita & PuTaxonomy of empathetic response intentsWhen do users prefer each intent?
Weak synthesis

“Paper A created a dataset. Paper B proposed a model. Paper C created a taxonomy.”

Strong synthesis

“The literature progressed from data, to emotion-conditioned generation, to interpretable response intentions. It still offers limited evidence about which intention users prefer in a given context.”

Technique 10 · Contradiction map

When findings disagree

Could both results be correct under different conditions?

Compare populations, tasks, exposure length, measures, and analytic choices. Contradiction often signals a moderator.

1
Screens, teens, and psychological well-being · Orben & Przybylski, 2019

Large datasets suggested small associations sensitive to analytic choices.

2
Facebook use and subjective well-being · Kross et al., 2013

Within-person longitudinal data linked use with later declines in well-being.

3
A preregistered replication of social media effects

Preregistered designs show how definitions and measurement windows can alter conclusions.

Workshop · Contradiction

The moderator hunt

  1. Write the two apparently conflicting claims.
  2. List differences in people, context, duration, and measure.
  3. Choose one difference as a moderator.
  4. Write a conditional hypothesis.
Weak

“Prior work is inconsistent.”

Strong

“AI assistance may improve immediate output but reduce later unaided performance when learners can copy answers without generating explanations.”

A contradiction becomes useful when it predicts where each result should occur.
Technique 11 · Novelty test

A gap that changes understanding

If answered, what belief, decision, or design would change?

Your knowledge gap is personal. A research gap is a documented uncertainty that matters to a research community or stakeholder.

Gap typePromptEvidence needed
MechanismWhy does the effect occur?Mediation, process data, qualitative evidence
BoundaryWhen or for whom does it change?Moderator or comparative setting
ContradictionWhy do credible studies disagree?Matched definitions and discriminating test
ConsequenceWhat happens later or elsewhere?Longitudinal or system-level outcome
Workshop · Novelty

The contribution sentence

Complete:

Prior work establishes ______. It remains unclear ______ because existing studies ______. We test ______, which matters because ______.

Then identify the three closest papers and state the exact difference from each.

Weak

“Few studies use this model in Thailand.”

Strong

“Prior studies measure immediate writing quality. We test whether repeated AI assistance changes unaided revision skill, separating short-term productivity from learning.”

Novelty is a precise difference plus a reason that difference matters.
Positioning checkpoint

Evidence before adjectives

  • Show what the closest work establishes.
  • Name the unresolved uncertainty without exaggerating “no one has studied.”
  • Explain why resolving it changes theory, method, design, or practice.
  • State the contribution as a result you can defend, not a feature list.
“First,” “novel,” and “comprehensive” never substitute for a comparison with prior work.

Technique 12 · Method–claim fit

What kind of claim?

What evidence would make a skeptical reader update their belief?

ClaimTypical methodMain threat
DescribeSurvey, corpus, observationCoverage and measurement
Explain experienceInterview, diary, field studyInterpretation and reflexivity
Estimate causal effectRandomized or strong quasi-experimentConfounding and noncompliance

1
Affective Diary

Research through design and field deployment explored emotional reflection.

2
Noy & Zhang

A preregistered experiment estimated effects on writing time and quality.

3
EmpatheticDialogues

Dataset creation, modeling, and human evaluation supported benchmark claims.

Workshop · Methods

The skeptical reviewer

  1. Underline the verb in your claim.
  2. Name the comparison implied by that verb.
  3. Write the strongest alternative explanation.
  4. Choose the least complex method that addresses it.
Weak

“We will use a survey because it is easy.”

Strong

“A survey can describe trust, but it cannot show that explanations cause trust. Random assignment to explanation conditions matches the causal claim.”

Choose the method after the claim and threat are explicit.
Technique 13 · Experiment design

A comparison that isolates the idea

What must remain equal so the focal difference can explain the result?

Define the unit, treatment, control, assignment, primary outcome, exclusion rules, and sample plan before seeing results.

1
Controlled experiments on the web · Kohavi et al., 2009

Large online experiments reveal both practical value and common pitfalls.

2
CONSORT statement

Randomized trials require transparent allocation, flow, outcomes, and harms.

3
Justify Your Alpha · Lakens et al., 2018

Design choices should follow the decision context rather than ritual thresholds.

Workshop · Experiments

The minimum convincing study

For your main claim, specify:

  • Unit and population
  • Treatment and active control
  • Primary outcome and time point
  • Randomization or matching
  • One manipulation check
  • One failure criterion
Weak

“Compare our system with ChatGPT.”

Strong

“Compare adaptive versus equally long nonadaptive feedback using the same base model, then test immediate accuracy and unaided transfer one week later.”

A fair control differs only in the mechanism your claim depends on.
Technique 14 · Analysis

Estimate, diagnose, interpret

What pattern would support the claim, and what pattern would weaken it?

Plan the estimand, uncertainty, assumptions, missing data, robustness checks, and qualitative coding before choosing attractive results.

1
ASA Statement on p-values · Wasserstein & Lazar, 2016

A p-value does not measure effect size, practical importance, or truth.

2
Moving to a world beyond p < .05 · Cumming, 2014 tradition

Estimation and intervals emphasize magnitude and uncertainty.

3
Using thematic analysis in psychology · Braun & Clarke, 2006

Qualitative analysis requires explicit analytic choices and a coherent relation between data and claims.

Workshop · Analysis

The result table before the results

  1. Draw the empty table or figure that would answer the question.
  2. Label the effect and uncertainty.
  3. Write the expected pattern.
  4. Write one null, opposite, or subgroup pattern and its interpretation.
Weak

“Run tests and see what is significant.”

Strong

“Estimate the adaptive-minus-control difference in transfer score with a confidence interval, then test the preregistered expertise interaction.”

Analysis answers a planned question. It does not search for a publishable pattern.
Technique 15 · Research storytelling

An argument with evidence

What must the reader believe before your contribution feels necessary?

  1. Context: establish the important situation.
  2. Problem: show the tension or consequence.
  3. Gap: identify the unresolved uncertainty.
  4. Approach: explain the test at the right level.
  5. Finding and contribution: state what changes in understanding.

Study the narrative moves in Vaswani et al., Noy & Zhang, and Rashkin et al.. Each motivates a specific limitation before presenting a method.

Workshop · Story

Five sentences

  1. People or systems ______.
  2. However, ______ causes ______.
  3. Prior work explains ______, but not ______.
  4. We compare or investigate ______.
  5. This reveals ______, which changes ______.
Weak

“We collected data, trained three models, and achieved 92% accuracy.”

Strong

“AI feedback can speed practice, but its effect on unaided transfer remains unclear. We isolate adaptivity while holding model and feedback length constant, then measure performance after access ends.”

Methods are plot events. The research question supplies the plot.
Complete study canvas

One page before months of work

  1. Observation or surprising pattern
  2. Research question with tension
  3. Closest literature and unresolved gap
  4. Claim and contribution
  1. Alternative explanation
  2. Method and fair comparison
  3. Primary outcome and analysis
  4. Failure criterion and next decision
If the pieces do not connect on one page, more implementation will not repair the logic.
Research practice

Ownership with frequent feedback

  • Meet your advisor early enough for small corrections.
  • Bring alternatives and a recommendation.
  • Read before claiming novelty.
  • Write while the idea is still changing.
  • Pilot the strongest claim first.
  • Keep decisions, failures, and rationale in a research log.
  • Use the lab's expertise and resources.
  • Report inconvenient results, not only supportive ones.
Show what you think should happen next and explain why.

Advanced · Synthesis library

Eight patterns for organizing literature

  1. Agreement: repeated result
  2. Contradiction: incompatible findings
  3. Progression: problem, improvement, remaining limit
  4. Methods: how design choices change conclusions
  1. Population: who is represented or missing
  2. Theory: competing mechanisms
  3. Boundary: where the effect changes
  4. Consequence: delayed or system-level outcomes
Choose the pattern that supports the argument. Do not force all eight into one review.
