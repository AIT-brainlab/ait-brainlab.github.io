How to Develop a Research Contribution

A self-paced tutorial and exercises covering idea generation, refinement, novelty validation, methodology testing, storytelling, and AI-assisted research

Use ← →, the buttons, or the session menu. Press F for fullscreen.

Choose a question worth answering

A useful research question connects importance, an unresolved issue, and a feasible way to learn.

AskBring to the discussion
Who needs the answer?A person, decision, or scientific understanding that could change.
What is still unknown?The closest papers and a precise difference that matters.
Can we learn it here?Available data, expertise, time, and a small first investigation.

Adapted from Hamming on important, tractable problems and Uri Alon on interest and feasibility.

Example: Turn a project into a question

Illustrative planning example — novelty and outcomes have not been established.

  1. Direction: Build an honest, adaptive sales agent.
  2. Question: Does explaining product limitations when they become relevant improve customer understanding?
  3. Competing explanation: Any improvement may come from more information or a longer conversation.
  4. Fair comparison: Compare adaptive and fixed explanations using the same verified facts and a comparable interaction budget.
  5. Useful outcome: Check understanding of exclusions and suitability against independently verified product facts; satisfaction alone is insufficient.

The contribution would explain when adaptation helps, where it fails, and what evidence supports that conclusion.

Exercise

The adaptive agent gives more correct answers. It also receives extra product information. Can we credit adaptation?

Extra information could explain the advantage. Give both agents the same verified facts and comparable opportunities to use them. If the advantage disappears, this test does not support a benefit from adaptation itself. It does not prove that adaptation can never help.

Use the research decision note. A shared document is enough. Choose tools to resolve a research uncertainty, rather than adding tools for their own sake.

For exploratory or design work, record the next observation or prototype comparison and what you hope to learn. A causal experiment is not required for every contribution.

Idea generation

Where could a good idea come from?

Idea generation

Four ways to find a starting idea

  1. Notice a problem. What problem do people still face?
  2. Notice a surprise. What happened differently from what you expected?
  3. Borrow a theory. Can a theory help explain a problem you care about?
  4. Extend a recent paper. What useful question could come next?

1. Notice a problem

Look at the people and situations around you. What problem do you notice repeatedly? When does it happen, and why does it matter?

1
Affective Diary · Lindström et al., CHI 2006
  • Problem: People may find it difficult to understand and reflect on their emotions.
  • When: This can happen when people cannot connect their feelings with what happened during the day.
  • Why it matters: Understanding emotions can help people manage their feelings and communicate with others.
  • Initial idea: Can we use bodily sensors to help people understand their emotions?
  • What the paper did: The researchers designed a diary that combines body data, personal notes, and pictures to support emotional reflection.

Idea refinement

How can we turn a broad idea into a strong research question?

Idea refinement

Five ways to make an idea stronger

  1. Challenge an assumption. What are you taking for granted?
  2. Consider another explanation. What else could cause the result?
  3. Use first principles. What must be true at the most basic level?
  4. Think about the whole system. How do the parts affect one another?
  5. Consider second-order effects. What may happen after the first benefit?

1. Challenge an assumption

Find what your question assumes is already true. Rewrite it so the assumption can be tested.

1
From Overload to Convergence · Bayesian reasoning example
  • Starting question: Can Bayesian reasoning help people make better decisions in complex negotiations?
  • Hidden assumption: If probabilities are mathematically correct, people will understand and use them correctly.
  • Challenge: People may misunderstand probabilities, trust them too much, or ignore information that is not shown.
  • Better question: When does Bayesian support improve negotiation decisions, and when does it create false confidence?
  • Why it is better: The question identifies a condition and a possible failure, rather than assuming the method is helpful.

2. Consider another explanation

Ask what else could produce the same result. Compare your explanation with the strongest alternative.

2
From Overload to Convergence · Bayesian reasoning example
  • Starting question: Does Bayesian visualization improve negotiation performance?
  • Expected explanation: Performance improves because Bayesian reasoning helps people understand uncertainty.
  • Alternative explanation: Any clear visual summary may reduce effort; the improvement may not come from Bayesian reasoning.
  • Better question: Does Bayesian information help beyond the benefit of a simpler non-Bayesian visualization?
  • Why it is better: It separates the proposed explanation from a simpler competing explanation.

3. Use first principles

Break the problem into its most basic needs, decisions, and constraints before choosing a method.

3
From Overload to Convergence · Bayesian reasoning example
  • Starting question: How can we use Bayesian reasoning to improve negotiation?
  • Break it down: Negotiation requires people to express preferences, compare trade-offs, handle uncertainty, and choose an offer.
  • Basic need: The user does not need “Bayesian reasoning” itself; the user needs help seeing uncertain trade-offs without losing control.
  • Better question: Which uncertain trade-offs should the system estimate, and how should it show them so people can still make the final choice?
  • Why it is better: It begins with the human decision problem, then selects mathematics only where it is useful.

4. Think about the whole system

Identify the people, technology, and feedback loops that influence one another over time.

4
From Overload to Convergence · Bayesian reasoning example
  • Starting question: Can Bayesian guidance help a person choose better negotiation offers?
  • Parts of the system: The person, AI agent, probability model, visualization, offers, and responses all influence one another.
  • Feedback loop: A probability display changes the next offer; that offer changes the AI response; the response changes the next probability.
  • Better question: How does Bayesian guidance change the negotiation over several rounds, including offers, trust, and the AI response?
  • Why it is better: It studies the changing interaction, not only one user choice or the final agreement.

5. Consider second-order effects

Look beyond the immediate benefit. Ask how people may adapt and what new effect may appear next.

5
From Overload to Convergence · Bayesian reasoning example
  • Starting question: Can Bayesian guidance help people reach an agreement faster?
  • First-order effect: People may identify promising offers and converge more quickly.
  • Second-order effect: They may depend on the guidance, explore fewer unusual offers, or overlook options outside the model.
  • Better question: Does Bayesian guidance improve convergence while reducing exploration or independent judgment?
  • Why it is better: It tests both the immediate benefit and the new problem that the benefit may create.

Idea validation

Is the idea truly novel and worth studying?

1. Validate the research gap

Your knowledge gap is not yet a research gap.

  1. Is there really a gap? Past work must leave an important question unanswered, inconsistent, or poorly supported.
  2. Is the gap in the field—not only in your reading? Start with strong CHI, ACL, and NeurIPS papers where relevant. Trace references and newer citing papers; search neighbouring fields and specialist venues. State what you searched and what uncertainty remains. A search cannot prove that no answer exists.
  3. Is it a clear next step? Aim for a focused advance that changes understanding or capability. A model swap, new domain, or combination of components needs evidence of a meaningful difference. You do not need a breakthrough.
  4. Is there a research contribution? Building an application or apparatus is not enough. It must answer a research question or produce evidence, design knowledge, or a tested technical capability.

04

Idea testing

What kind of contribution will the paper make?

Choose the contribution first

Different contributions need different methodologies

  1. Experimental: evidence about an effect or causal relationship.
  2. Design research: design knowledge, principles, or rationale.
  3. Artifact or system: a working technical capability.
  4. Exploratory qualitative: understanding of a new situation.
  5. Model or dataset: measurable computational performance and limitations.

Check three kinds of validity

Internal validity

Did X really cause the change in Y?

Check: confounds, controls, randomization, counterbalancing, and alternative explanations.

Construct validity

Did you measure the concept you claim to study?

Check: operational definitions, validated measures, manipulation checks, and whether the task represents the construct.

External validity

Where should the result generalize?

Check: participants, tasks, settings, realism, cultural context, and replication.

Experimental contribution

Typical question: Does X affect Y? Use an experiment when the contribution is evidence that an independent variable changes a dependent variable.

  • Specify: IV and levels; DV and operational measure; between-, within-, or mixed design; task; procedure; sample; randomization or counterbalancing; controls and likely confounds.
  • Typical evidence and analysis: Descriptive plots; t-test; ANOVA; regression or mixed-effects models; effect sizes, confidence intervals, manipulation checks, and assumption checks.

Example: Impact of User Mobility on Attentional Tunneling in Handheld AR · Parmar & Silpasuwanchai, CHI EA 2023

Design research contribution

Typical question: What design knowledge can we produce? The contribution is theory-informed rationale, principles, tensions, or implications—not merely proof that one interface is better.

  • Specify: human need; grounding theory; design goals; alternatives; rationale; iterations; context; and what transfers beyond the artifact.
  • Typical evidence and analysis: Formative interviews or observations; co-design; workshops; diaries; field deployment; think-aloud; thematic analysis; reflection across iterations and triangulation.

Example: A Framework for Interactive Mindfulness Meditation Using Attention-Regulation Process · Niksirat et al., CHI 2017

Artifact or system contribution

Typical question: What new capability does the artifact provide? Its evaluation should show that it works, can be used, and meets the technical requirements connected to its claim.

  • Specify: capability; architecture; implementation; intended users and tasks; baseline; success criteria; limitations and deployment constraints.
  • Typical evidence and analysis: Accuracy, latency, reliability, scalability, or resource use; usability and task performance; interviews or observations; benchmark comparison and ablation when components matter.

Example: From Overload to Convergence: Supporting Multi-Issue Human–AI Negotiation with Bayesian Visualization · CHI 2026

Exploratory qualitative contribution

Typical question: What is happening in this new situation? This approach fits a topic, community, behavior, or technology that is too new for precise variables or hypotheses.

  • Specify: phenomenon and context; why exploration is needed; purposeful participant selection; researcher position; protocol; how interpretations will be checked.
  • Typical evidence and analysis: Semi-structured interviews, observations, fieldwork, diaries, focus groups, or documents; thematic analysis, grounded theory, or qualitative content analysis; negative cases, reflexivity and triangulation.

Example: Understanding the Role of Human Senses in Interactive Meditation · Ahmed et al., CHI 2017

Model or dataset contribution

Typical question: How well does it work—and where does it fail? Average scores are insufficient: evaluation must expose meaningful strengths and weaknesses.

  • Specify: data source and representativeness; train/validation/test separation; metrics; strong baselines; reproducibility; leakage, bias, and ethical risks.
  • Typical evidence and analysis: Benchmark comparison; statistical uncertainty; ablations; error analysis; robustness and stress tests; subgroup analysis; human evaluation when automated metrics miss quality.

Example: Comparing Selective Masking Methods for Depression Detection in Social Media · Pananookooln et al., Computational Linguistics 2023

Summary: Idea testing

Study a strong paper with a similar contribution. Explain why its task, sample, measures, and analysis fit your question before adapting them. Use a credible simple comparison and ask what alternative explanation remains. A published method is a starting point, not a guarantee of validity.

Idea storytelling

How can readers follow one clear research story?

People remember stories

A research paper is easier to understand and remember when five parts form one connected story. The problem is the turning point: it moves readers from what is known to what your study must do.

  1. Background: Establish the important topic and synthesize what is known.
  2. Problem: Turn the story with “However.”
  3. Methodology: Explain how the paper answers the problem.
  4. Findings: Reveal what the evidence shows.
  5. Contribution / significance: End with what changes because of the work.

Tip—choose the verb carefully: investigates a relationship; explores a new situation; proposes an idea; develops an artifact; evaluates performance or usefulness.

One reusable template

Write the story in five parts

  1. Background: Emotion regulation is important because … Prior work shows … (use many relevant citations)
  2. Problem: However, existing digital meditation tools mainly use spoken instructions or fixed content, giving users limited ways to regulate attention through their own action.
  3. Methodology: This paper proposes an attention-regulation framework and develops Pause, an interaction based on slow, continuous movement.
  4. Findings: The key findings are …
  5. Contribution / significance: This paper contributes … This matters because …

Your title is the first line of the story

Mediocre title

“A Study of a Meditation Application”

Too broad. Readers cannot see the mechanism, contribution, or research area.

Stronger title

“Pause: Using Slow, Continuous Movement to Support Attention Regulation in Interactive Mindfulness Meditation”

Memorable hook: Pause. Strong keywords: movement, attention regulation, interactive mindfulness meditation.

Use clear research keywords so the right readers can find the paper. A short, meaningful hook can help them remember it.

Example: Attention regulation as one connected story

A Framework for Interactive Mindfulness Meditation Using Attention-Regulation Process · Niksirat et al., CHI 2017
  • Background: Emotion regulation supports mental well-being, and mindfulness is widely used to train attention and regulate emotion.
  • Problem: However, existing digital meditation tools mainly use spoken instructions or fixed content, giving users limited ways to regulate attention through their own action.
  • Methodology: The paper proposes an attention-regulation framework, develops Pause, and evaluates the movement-based interaction with users.
  • Findings: Slow, continuous interaction supported attention and produced positive affective and engagement outcomes.
  • Contribution / significance: The paper contributes a framework and design approach for self-regulated mindfulness technologies.

Bonus: AI for research

Use AI to challenge, express, visualize, and review your thinking.

Four ways AI can help your research

  1. Test and refine the novelty. Search, judge, improve the idea, and search again.
  2. Build the paper slowly. Start with five sentences, then develop one section at a time.
  3. Draw the research. Make the contribution and methodology easy to understand.
  4. Review the work. Check novelty, validity, and communication.

Put AI into the whole research loop

  1. Write the question and study plan early. Draft the introduction, related work, and proposed methodology. Keep expected outcomes in a separate planning note. Leave result tables empty until real evidence is available.
  2. Build and run the study. For computational work, ask AI to help implement and run the experiments. For human studies, ask it to help prepare the apparatus, tasks, protocol, measures, and analysis plan.
  3. Connect claims to evidence. Add the real results to the paper. Revise the claims and discussion, and ask what additional experiments or analyses are needed.
  4. Loop between writing and experiments. Let weaknesses in the paper suggest the next experiment; let new evidence reshape the paper. Keep exploratory analyses separate from planned tests. Log changes and failures; do not keep changing the evaluation until a preferred result appears.
  5. Run the final checks. Check novelty; construct, internal, and external validity; coherence; terminology; overclaims; and writing quality. Fix the most serious problem first and repeat.
  6. Choose the venue and prepare submission. Identify a suitable journal or conference. Ask AI agents to prepare the files and submission information, but verify every claim, author detail, policy, and uploaded file before you authorize submission.

1. Test and refine the novelty

Search, judge the idea honestly, improve it, and repeat until the contribution is clear.

  • Search widely: Check nearby terms, fields, theories, methods, and papers.
  • Judge: Not novel, a distinction needing verification, or a supported distinction within the searched literature.
  • Refine: Challenge assumptions and rethink the idea from several angles.
Prompt
My research question or topic is: [your question or topic idea]. My target venue is [CHI / ACL / NeurIPS / other].

Work with me in a repeated novelty loop:
1. Find nearby terms, neighboring fields, related theories, and the closest papers. Give me searches I can run and links I can verify.
2. Compare my idea with the verified papers. Judge it as substantial overlap, a possible distinction needing verification, or a supported distinction within the searched literature. Explain the evidence and uncertainty behind the judgment.
3. Suggest several stronger versions of the idea. Use challenging assumptions, alternative explanations, first-principles thinking, systems thinking, and second-order effects.
4. For each version, explain what changed, why it may be more novel, and what new evidence would be needed.
5. Ask me which version to continue. Then repeat Steps 1–4 until the research gap and contribution are convincing.

Do not invent papers. Clearly mark anything I still need to verify.

4. Ask AI to review the paper

Run three focused checks. Fix the most serious concern, then check again.

Novelty check

Is the gap real? Is the contribution clearly different from the closest verified work?

Validity check

Are construct, internal, and external validity strong enough for the claims?

Communication check

Is the story coherent, consistent, clear, and free from overclaiming?

Act as a critical reviewer for [target venue]. Review my paper in three separate passes.

Novelty: Compare the claimed gap and contribution with the closest verified papers. Judge the novelty as substantial overlap, a possible distinction needing verification, or a supported distinction within the searched literature, and explain why.
Validity: Check construct validity, internal validity, and external validity. Identify weak measures, confounds, unfair comparisons, unsupported generalization, and analyses that do not support the claims.
Communication: Check coherence, conflicting statements, confusing or inconsistent terms, missing argument links, and overclaims.

For every concern, quote the exact evidence, label it Major or Minor, and propose a concrete fix. Rank the three concerns most likely to affect acceptance. Let me revise them one at a time; after each revision, check whether the concern is resolved.

Keep yourself responsible

AI may assist

Search planning, outlining, language revision, diagrams, code explanation, alternative explanations, and reviewer simulation.

You must verify

Citations, novelty, data, analysis, figures, interpretations, authorship, disclosure, and the target venue's current AI policy.

  • Never upload: identifiable participant data, confidential reviews, or protected project material without authorization.
  • Never accept: invented papers, fabricated results, silent changes to numbers, or claims unsupported by your evidence.
  • Keep a record: save important prompts, outputs, decisions, and human corrections when reproducibility or disclosure matters.

Which journal should I submit to?

  1. Match the journal's scope. Read recent papers. Choose a journal that already publishes your topic, contribution, and methodology.
  2. Decide between open access and free publication. Check publication fees, institutional agreements, and whether readers can access the paper.
  3. Choose the audience for your contribution. Read recent papers and check the journal’s scope, review criteria, and author guidance.
  4. Consider a leading conference first. In HCI, NLP, and machine learning, CHI, ACL, NeurIPS, and ICML are often stronger first targets for timely original work.

Venue reputation does not establish the validity of a paper. Scope, contribution, and evidence come first.

Research process

Progress through evidence and revision

A novice tries to avoid failure. An expert has learned through many more attempts, criticism, and revisions.

  • Start with something you are curious about.
  • Read top-tier papers, especially from CHI, ACL, and NeurIPS.
  • Use feedback and failures to revise. Ask which assumption changed and what you should test next. You may disagree with feedback when you can explain why.
  • Prioritize the next important step. Do not try to solve everything at once.
  • Think before meeting your professor. Bring your interpretation, proposed decision, and next step—not only information.

A useful meeting ends with an evidence-based decision, a feasible next action, and the support needed to take it.
