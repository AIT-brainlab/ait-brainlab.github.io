1. **Treat research as an iterative process. Progress over perfection.** Read, think, build, test, write, and repeat. Each round should improve your understanding; you do not need to get everything right the first time.

2. **Keep writing, or you will get lost.** Write your question, reasoning, evidence, and next step as the work develops. Writing helps you see what you understand and what is still missing.

3. **Put novelty and validity first.** What is new compared with the closest work? Does your evidence support what you claim? A complicated system cannot compensate for a weak answer to either question.

4. **Read top-tier papers first, especially peer-reviewed work.** Learn how strong researchers frame questions, design studies, and support their conclusions. Then follow the relevant specialist work and recent preprints; a venue name alone does not make a claim correct.

5. **Do what you are really curious about.** Choose a question you want to understand, not just a fashionable tool you want to use. Curiosity gives you a reason to keep digging when the easy answers run out.

6. **Your first question is usually not your best question.** Challenge yourself: Why does this matter? Has someone already answered it? Is there a simpler explanation? Keep refining the question as you learn.

7. **Avoid topics with too many unknowns.** If the project depends on getting a new dataset, inventing a method, and figuring out how to evaluate it, each unknown can delay the whole study. Keep the question you care about and narrow it until you can run a first test with the time, data, and tools you have.

8. **Your knowledge gap may not be a research gap.** Read the closest papers and write down what they already answer and what important question they leave open. If they already answer your question, use what you learned to ask a better one.

9. **Go deep on one question, not broad.** Building a complete application can take months and still leave your research contribution unclear. Pick **one narrow question**, build only what you need to answer it, and spend the rest of your effort understanding the result.

   - **Too broad:** “I will build an AI insurance sales assistant that recommends plans, answers questions, and handles purchases.” This describes features. What will we learn from the study?
   - **Narrow and deep:** “When an insurance plan is unsuitable, does explaining why help customers correctly decide to reject it?” Compare a warning alone with the same warning plus an explanation. Measure whether customers make the right decision, then examine the cases where the explanation helps or fails and look for evidence of why.

   **Before you build, finish this sentence:** “My study will answer ___ by comparing ___ and measuring ___.” Then ask what the result would teach someone who never uses your app. Cut features that do not help answer the question.

10. **Search GitHub before building from scratch.** Look for code from the closest papers, existing baselines, and evaluation scripts. Check the license and reproduce a small result before building on the code.

11. **Keep thinking about factors and outcomes: IVs and DVs.** What exactly do you want to study or change? What outcome will you measure? What is your hypothesis, and what result would challenge it? Define success before looking at the results.

12. **Learn how strong papers frame their argument.** Study their structure and logic, then write in your own words. Every paper has limitations; persuasive writing makes the contribution and its boundaries clear. It does not hide weak evidence.

13. **Put your soul into it.** Research needs curiosity, focus, and care. If you work randomly without thinking deeply about the question, you are unlikely to get there. Work consistently, reflect honestly, and let what you learn guide your next step.
