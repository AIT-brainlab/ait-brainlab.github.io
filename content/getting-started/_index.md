You can propose your own idea or join an [available project](/available-projects/). You do not need to arrive with a polished or proven-novel topic. Bring a question you care about and be ready to develop it through reading, discussion, and a small first investigation.

## 1. Choose a Starting Point

- **Your own idea:** Explain the problem you have noticed and why it interests you.
- **An existing project:** Identify the project and the part you would like to explore. You can begin with a project-interest note if the research question is not yet clear.

Review our [Research Areas](/projects/) and note the skills you already have, what you need to learn, your degree, and the time available.

## 2. Prepare a One-Page Idea

Use the following **AIT Brain Lab discussion outline** for an initial one-page note. This is a starting outline for lab discussion, not an official AIT thesis-proposal form. Formal proposal documents should follow your programme's requirements and the templates in our [Research Guide](/researchguide/).

Include a working title, your name, degree, and proposed project area. Keep the main note to about one page; list references separately if needed.

| Part | What to write |
| --- | --- |
| Background | What is the context? What do existing approaches do? Cite the closest relevant work you have read. |
| Problem | What remains difficult, unexplained, or inadequately supported? Who is affected, and why does it matter? |
| Research question | What specific question will this project try to answer? |
| Proposed solution or approach | What might you build, investigate, compare, or analyse? Explain why the approach could help. |
| Evaluation | What evidence would answer the question? Identify a comparison, data or participants, measures, and important limitations. |
| Expected contribution | What could we learn or gain beyond completing an application? Label this as an expectation, not a result. |
| Feasibility and first step | What resources and skills are needed? What is the smallest useful investigation you could start with? |

> It is fine to write “I do not know yet.” Distinguish what you have verified from your assumptions. Do not invent references, results, or claims of novelty.

For example, “build an AI sales agent” is a starting direction. A more focused question could be: “Does explicitly stating product limitations improve customers’ understanding and decision quality compared with an agent that only describes benefits?” This is an illustrative question; its novelty and feasibility still need checking.

## 3. Discuss and Refine Together

Bring your note and the closest papers to the first project discussion. We will consider the importance of the problem, overlap with prior work, fit with the lab, available resources, and an appropriate scope.

- **PhD:** Work toward an original contribution and a research direction you can increasingly lead.
- **Master’s:** Define an identifiable piece of work, usually within a PhD or grant project, that meets your learning goals and degree requirements.

The outcome may be a revised question, a short literature review, a baseline reproduction, or a small feasibility study. We will agree on the next deliverable and when to review it.

## 4. Working Together

### Meeting Frequency

We meet more frequently while the research direction is taking shape, then adjust as the project develops and the student becomes more independent.

- **Early stage: weekly.** Use frequent discussions to refine the question, assess prior work, and plan a feasible study.
- **Once the direction and plan are established: every two weeks.** Review evidence, resolve bottlenecks, and agree on the next steps.
- **Later, when the work is progressing independently: on demand.** Request a meeting when a decision, difficulty, or important milestone needs discussion.

We agree together when to change the meeting frequency. If you become stuck, ask for help promptly rather than waiting for the next scheduled meeting.

### Read, Question, and Write Early

Read strong papers from leading venues such as **ACL, CHI, and NeurIPS**, choosing work relevant to your research question and contribution. Examine how the authors identify an important gap, formulate a strong question, design their study, and support their claims.

Use this reading to identify and refine your own research questions. Explain what the closest work already answers, what remains unresolved, and why your question matters.

Begin drafting the **first three chapters** early, following your programme's required structure. These will usually cover:

1. **Introduction:** Background, problem, research questions, objectives, and expected contribution.
2. **Literature review:** A critical synthesis of the closest work and the gap your project addresses.
3. **Methodology:** The proposed approach, study or system design, evaluation, analysis, and feasibility.

Treat these chapters as working documents. Revise them as your reading, discussions, and early experiments change your understanding. Planned methods and expected contributions should be clearly distinguished from completed work and actual findings.

### Bring Your Thinking to Meetings

A progress discussion should help us make decisions and move the project forward. Before a meeting, prepare:

- **Progress and evidence:** What did you try, and what did you find?
- **Interpretation:** What do you think the evidence means? What remains uncertain?
- **Bottleneck:** What is preventing progress, and what have you already tried to resolve it?
- **Proposed next steps:** What do you recommend doing next, and why? Bring alternatives when useful.
- **Support needed:** What decision, feedback, or resource do you need from the supervisor or team?

Present information together with your interpretation and a proposed action. You do not need to have the answer already; identifying the difficulty clearly is a useful contribution to the discussion.

### When the Project Gets Stuck

Our first approach is to **keep the same overall topic and adjust the project so it can continue**. We may narrow the question, change the method, use more suitable data, simplify the system, or revise the evaluation. Together, we choose a feasible next step that preserves a meaningful contribution and meets the degree requirements.

Raise bottlenecks early so we can make these adjustments before they threaten completion.

### Before Booking a Proposal or Defense

**Send the report to the supervisor before booking your proposal presentation or final defense.** Allow time for final comments and any necessary revisions. Agree on the review timeline with the supervisor before making booking arrangements, and follow the programme's formal procedures and deadlines.

### Shared Responsibilities

Agree on communication, feedback expectations, responsibilities, access to resources, and milestones. Discuss individual contributions and authorship early and revisit them as the work develops.

Use the relevant [Scorecard](/scorecards/) when it helps identify the next one or two priorities. Consult the [Research Guide](/researchguide/) for practical help with ideas, novelty, methodology, and writing.
